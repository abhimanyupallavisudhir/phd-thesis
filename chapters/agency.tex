\chapter{Price recursion as the rational theory of reward}\label{ch:agency}

\chaptersource{This chapter is based on the informal articles
``\href{https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/z7DBBn9RaDyKJRidg/reward-is-hyperstitional-information}{Reward
is hyperstitional information}'' and ``Price recursion is the rational theory of reward''
(originally titled ``Markets are equivalent to stuff''), written as part of the author's work
at MATS~10.0 in Richard Ngo's stream, together with the accompanying research notes
(\texttt{THEORY.md}, \texttt{ATTENTION.md}) and the \texttt{marketgrad} library
(Section~\ref{agency:sec:library}) developed there.}

\chapterabstract{\draft{Rational behaviour for a purely predictive agent is completely
specified by Bayes's theorem; but for any agent that acts in the world there is an arbitrary
thing called the ``utility function'', shaped by an equally arbitrary reward. This chapter
develops an economic answer to where reward comes from. First, entropic mirror ascent ---
the natural optimization dynamic on probability space --- is shown to be continuous-time
Bayesian inference, so that reward is (hyperstitional) information. Then, in a general model
of a production economy, shadow prices are shown to obey a recursion that is simultaneously
backpropagation and Bellman recursion: chain economies are exactly multi-layer perceptrons,
markets are exactly MDPs with matrix-valued discount factors, and entropic mirror ascent on
routing shares is exactly replicator dynamics/continuous Bayesian inference, with revenue as
the evidence rate. The economy is thus the ``super-agent'' that generates reward for its
sub-agents, and price recursion is the rational theory of reward. Supporting results
(the advantage identity, the fundamental theorem of welfare dynamics, the market--attention
adjunction, and the decentralization of backprop into a market protocol) and the
\texttt{marketgrad} library that numerically verifies them are included.}}

A bunch of people have vaguely noticed intuitive analogies between economies/markets and
machine learning algorithms:
\href{https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/brhWPoNsBN7za3xjs/competitive-markets-as-distributed-backprop}{John Wentworth},
Aanjaneya Kumar and Anders Sandberg at the
\href{https://ai.objectives.institute/projects}{AI Objectives institute}, the ancient Neural
Bucket Brigade
(\href{https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/1988/hash/82aa4b0af34c2313a562076992e50aa3-Abstract.html}{Davis},
\href{https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/09540098908915650}{Schmidhuber}). Several
papers discuss RL algorithms based on sequentially auctioning a unit ``right to act'' among
agents---most recently \href{https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.05068v1}{Caspar Oesterheld's BRIA}
but also Baum's \href{https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/a:1007593124513}{Hayek
Machine}, its predecessors the classifier systems (Holland 1985,
\href{https://mediatum.ub.tum.de/813180}{Schmidhuber 1987},
\href{https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/09540098908915650}{Schmidhuber 1989}) and
its successors Hayek-for-POMDPs
(\href{https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/3-540-44668-0_120}{Kwee et al 2001}) and
Hayek with Vickrey auctions
(\href{https://proceedings.mlr.press/v119/chang20b.html}{Chang et al 2020}). In
``\href{https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.05828}{Market-based architectures in RL and beyond}''
I suggested that a better market model for RL might come out of factoring the ``right to
act'' into ``goods'' axes, just like in real markets.

The analogy is attractive and suggestive:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Economies are intelligent agents built out of sub-agents much less capable than
  themselves (see e.g.\ ``I, Pencil'') suggesting that markets are an example of
  intelligence being an ``emergent phenomenon''.
  \item Bounded rationality is very analogous to the Efficient Market Hypothesis. How can
  you be rational when computing the rational decision itself has a cost---how do you
  optimize over that? You are rational/markets are efficient ``conditional on the
  information (including computational information) present in the system'', whatever that
  means.
  \item Markets have analogs of inconsistency (arbitrage) and
  \href{https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/xqxXrAohXSD3akYCg/meaningful-things-are-those-the-universe-possesses-a}{G\"odel's
  incompleteness theorem}: a prediction market is limited in its ability to efficiently
  price the question ``This question will resolve YES if this market gives less than 50\%
  probability to it''. (More specifically, a market must give zero subsidy to such a
  question: its subjective ``value for information'' on that question must be zero.)
  \item Related to 3: ``Market dynamics'' is famously under-studied because again it is
  fundamentally computational and boundedly-rational rather than ``there exists an
  equilibrium, DONE''.
  The dynamical nature of markets feels very similar to backpropagation (as well as e.g.\
  Bellman recursion): prices are gradients (specifically: gradients on activations, not
  weights---they do not zero out at equilibrium).
  %% TODO(agency): the source sentence trails off here ("..., the gradient"); completed
  %% minimally by closing the parenthetical.
\end{enumerate}

In this chapter we will formalize this intuition, and explain that in fact, markets are
equivalent to a lot of things: backprop, RL, and continuous Bayesian inference (aka
``replicators'').

\section{Bregman divergences, mirror ascent and reward as hyperstitious information}
\label{agency:sec:hyperstition}
% Source: marketgrad-theory/Bregman divergences, mirror ascent and reward as
% hyperstitious information.md (the precedent article, lifted in full).

The logarithmic scoring rule $b\log q(x)$
\href{https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/fCGXK7oyhM4ei77gt/lmsr-subsidy-parameter-is-the-price-of-information}{exactly
puts a price on information by the usual measure}: the expected score an agent with some
belief distribution $q$ gets is exactly $-bH(p,q)$ where $p$ is the true distribution (or,
more accurately: the score we expect that agent to get is $-bH(p,q)$, from our
perspective/belief $p$).

Consequently, the value of some information $Y$ is precisely its mutual information
$bI(X;Y)$ with the event of interest, etc.

Here's a question: \textbf{does every proper scoring rule correspond to an information
measure?} For instance, does the quadratic scoring rule incentivize obtaining information in
the sense of ``quadratic negentropy'' much like logarithmic scoring incentivizes obtaining
information in the sense of ``logarithmic negentropy''?

\subsection{Generalized entropies and Bregman divergence}\label{agency:sec:bregman}

A scoring rule is some function $S(x, q)$ that tells you how much an agent gets for belief
$q$ when an outcome $x$ is realized---equivalently that is how someone with belief
$\delta_x$ would score belief $q$. This also tells you how someone with belief $p$ would
score belief $q$:
$$G(p,q)=\sum_x p(x)S(x,q)$$
Properness means that $q=p$ maximizes it, i.e.
$$G(p):=G(p,p)=\max_q G(p,q)$$
$G(p)$ is the score we give to our own beliefs, thus there is no bias penalty, only an
uncertainty penalty: it measures our ``certainty'' about our own beliefs.

(So $G(p)$ and $G(p,q)$ are the generalized ``negentropy'' and ``cross-negentropy''
respectively, and their negatives are the generalized entropy and cross-entropy.)

Now observe: fix $q$ and look at the maps $p\mapsto G(p)$ and $p\mapsto G(p,q)$. The latter
is linear in $p$, and they touch at the point $p=q$ (everywhere else $G(p,q)$ lies below
$G(p)$).

Thus for every $q$, $G(p,q)$ is the tangent plane under the graph of $G(p)$ at the point
$p=q$. In other words, \textbf{any (neg-)entropy function $G(p)$ naturally induces a cross
(neg-)entropy function $G(p,q)$} by its tangent planes:
$$G(p,q)=G(q)+\langle\nabla G(q), p-q\rangle$$

When $G(p)$ is convex, i.e.\ it sits above all its tangent planes (in fact it is exactly the
max of all its tangent planes), the resulting scoring rule is proper i.e.\
$G(p)=\max_q G(p,q)$. \textbf{Thus every convex function generates an information theory.}

So the Generalized Scoring $S(x,q)=G(\delta_x, q)$, the Generalized Cross-Entropy $-G(p,q)$
and the Generalized Entropy $-G(p)$ are all equally fundamental; you can derive the rest
from any one of them.

We also define the Generalized KL-divergence (``Bregman Divergence'') as the vertical gap
between $G(p)$ and the tangent plane $G(p,q)$, i.e.\ ``how much does $p$ believe itself to
be better than $q$'':
$$D(p\parallel q)=G(p)-G(p,q)$$

For log score $S(x, q)=\log q(x)$, we have usual information theory. For the quadratic score
$S(x,q)=-\|q-\delta_x\|^2$ we have cross-negentropy $G(p,q)=\langle p, q\rangle-\|q\|^2-1$,
negentropy $G(p)=\|p\|^2-1$ (the negative of which, the quadratic entropy, is called the
``Gini impurity'') and Bregman divergence $D(p\parallel q)=\|p-q\|^2$.

An entropy function inducing a cross-entropy function may remind you of how norms naturally
induce inner products (polarization identity). So is cross-entropy an inner product? Not
really. If you take a quadratic form $G(p)=\frac12 \langle p, A p\rangle$ (differing from
the above quadratic entropy by constant term and factor) then $\nabla H(p)=Ap$, and
$$H(p,q)= \frac12 \langle q, A q\rangle+\langle Aq, p-q\rangle=\langle p,Aq\rangle-\frac12 \langle q,Aq\rangle$$
Which is not the inner product, but it does suggest a generalized ``inner product'' of
distributions: $\langle p, q\rangle =G(p,q)+G(q)=G(p)+G(q)-D(p\parallel q)$. But it is not
in general symmetric, etc.

\subsection{Mirror ascent}\label{agency:sec:mirror}

What exactly is gradient ascent?
\begin{quote}
``Climbing in the direction of steepest ascent''
\end{quote}
ok, what is ``the direction of steepest ascent?''
\begin{quote}
Among all $\varepsilon$-steps in each direction, we choose the $\varepsilon$-step that gives
the highest rise in the objective (as measured by a first-order Taylor approximation of the
objective, i.e.\ the gradient)
\end{quote}

I.e.\ given a current value $x_t$, we want to find the $x$ that maximizes $f(x)-f(x_t)$. The
first-order Taylor approximation for this is $\langle\nabla f(x_t), x-x_t\rangle$---but we
cannot trust the first-order Taylor approximation if we move too far, so we restrict the
search to an $\varepsilon$-neighbourhood around $x_t$. Thus:
$$x_{t+1}=\argmax_x \langle\nabla f(x_t), x-x_t\rangle, \quad D(x, x_t)\le\varepsilon$$

This is a constrained optimization problem, so define a Lagrangian:
$$\mathcal{L}(x,\lambda)= \langle\nabla f(x_t), x-x_t\rangle - \lambda (D(x, x_t)-\varepsilon)$$

Differentiating we get:
\begin{equation}
\nabla D(x_{t+1}, x_t) = \frac1\lambda \nabla f(x_t) \tag{$*$}
\end{equation}
$$D(x_{t+1}, x_t)=\varepsilon$$

For the usual Euclidean metric, $\nabla D(x_{t+1}, x_t) = 2(x_{t+1}-x_t)$ so we can solve
and get usual gradient ascent:
$$x_{t+1} = x_t + \eta \nabla f(x_t)$$

Instead suppose $D(x_{t+1}, x_t)$ is an arbitrary Bregman divergence, i.e.\
$D(x, y) = G(x) - G(y) - \langle \nabla G(y), x-y\rangle$ for convex $G$. The gradient of
this with respect to its first parameter is simply
$$\nabla_x D(x, y) = \nabla G(x) - \nabla G(y)$$

So plugging this back into $(*)$ we get the update rule:
\begin{equation}
\nabla G(x_{t+1}) = \nabla G(x_t) + \eta\nabla f(x_t)\tag{$**$}
\end{equation}

For convex $G$, there is a one-to-one map between the values of $x$ and the values of
$\nabla G(x)$; i.e.\ the gradients live in a ``$G$-dual space'' or \textbf{mirror space} and
this amounts exactly to doing gradient ascent in the mirror space, i.e.\ ``mirror ascent''.

For $G(x)= \|x\|^2$ (the usual quadratic metric), the map is the identity (scaled by $2$),
and---

\subsection{Reward is hyperstitional information}\label{agency:sec:hyperstitional}

for $G(p)=p\cdot\log(p)$ we have $\nabla G(p) = 1+\log(p)$ thus the update rule is
multiplicative (\textbf{entropic mirror ascent}):
$$p_{t+1} \propto p_t\exp\left(\eta\nabla f(p_t)\right)$$

(we are allowed to normalize because for probabilities the original problem is itself a
constrained optimization problem with a simplex constraint)

In other words: \textbf{optimizing some objective $f(p)$ over probability space with
entropic mirror ascent, is continuous-time Bayesian inference with likelihood function
$\exp\left(\eta\nabla f(p)\right)$}.

Unlike usual Bayesian inference:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item this likelihood function depends on the current $p$
  \item this likelihood function can be \ldots\ anything.
\end{enumerate}

From a predictive processing/active inference perspective, all rational behaviour must be
Bayesian inference, and utility functions are fake. What you see as ``utility
functions''/goals are actually the agent's prior: \textbf{and inference arbitrages between
the agent's beliefs and the environment} (when the environment's information updates the
agent that is ``learning''; when the agent's information influences the environment that is
``action'').

And similarly, now we have that reward is also, information. What is it information about?
Well, an RL agent produces probabilities $\pi(x)$ for its actions and samples from them (so
the policy also functions as the agent's beliefs about its own actions), and reward updates
these probabilities---so it is simply information about ``what I will do'', and is
definitionally rational, because ``what I will do'' is \emph{actually} updated and the
agent's beliefs about it are updated in-sync. You can specify \emph{any} objective
whatsoever, and optimizing it (at least through entropic mirror ascent) will still be
rational Bayesian inference. In other words, reward is \emph{hyperstitional} information.

(And if you multiply all the exponentiated rewards the agent gets through its training, you
get its final prior, which expresses its exponentiated utility function. Kinda, maybe,
oversimplified, whatever.)

In bandits, the objective is just the reward; in more general RL you must use Q-values, and
also do importance sampling (i.e.\ use $f(p)/p_i$ where $i$ is the taken action instead of
$f(p)$) because you only have access to the reward for one performed action. PPO methods
used in modern RL also are approximately entropic mirror ascent (TRPO is exactly it; other
stuff is kinda). You may also recall that evolution (replicator dynamics) is Bayesian
inference (\href{https://arxiv.org/abs/0911.1763}{Harper 2009}, explained by
\href{https://johncarlosbaez.wordpress.com/2012/06/01/information-geometry-part-9/}{John
Baez here}). Indeed, it is exactly equivalent to this dynamic, with the fitness function as
an exponentiated reward.

\subsection{Continuous-time mirror ascent and information geometry}\label{agency:sec:infogeom}

It is interesting to take the continuous limit of this dynamics. We have:
$$\frac{d}{dt}\nabla G(x)= \nabla f(x)$$

(i.e.\ the velocity in the mirror space is the gradient of your objective function). What
does this look like in the original primal space? Well we can apply the chain rule to
rewrite the left side as $\nabla^2 G(x)\frac{dx}{dt}$ (where $\nabla^2$ is the Hessian)
thus:
\begin{equation}
\frac{dx}{dt}=[\nabla^2 G(x)]^{-1}\nabla f(x)\tag{$*\!*\!*$}
\end{equation}

This gives us a very natural interpretation of mirror ascent: if you let the Hessian
$\nabla^2 G(x)$ define a Riemannian metric on your space, \textbf{then mirror ascent is
simply gradient ascent of the objective $f(x)$ on that geometry.} Physicists are of course
quite used to transforming between primal and dual spaces by multiplying by the metric and
its inverse respectively.

Pitfall prevention: this is not gravity, nor is it any Newtonian force (as that would drive
the acceleration not the velocity).

When $G(x)$ is entropy, its Hessian is called the ``Fisher information matrix'' or
``Fisher--Rao metric'' $\diag(1/p)$ and the geometry defined by this Riemannian metric is
called information geometry.

\subsection{Advantage is rivalry}\label{agency:sec:advantage-rivalry}

Actually, the rule in the above section is not exactly right for the probabilistic case,
because it does not account for the probability normalization (i.e.\ the fact that the
original optimization itself has a constraint of probabilities adding up to 1).

You can just take the continuous limit of the multiplicative update rule directly.

In Bayesian inference, imagine the likelihood function to arrive over a period of time, so
that over time $dt$, you get infinitesimal evidence $\exp(v\ dt)$. Call $v$ the ``rate of
evidence'' (and it is a vector where each index is a hypothesis)---and $\bar{v}=p\cdot v$ is
your ``expected evidence rate''. Then (where $\odot$ is the pointwise product of vectors)
Bayes's theorem is:
$$p(t+dt)=\frac{p(t)\odot\exp(v\ dt)}{p(t)\cdot \exp(v\ dt)}$$

Take first-order approximations $\exp(x)\approx 1 + x$ and $\frac1{1+x}\approx 1-x$:
\begin{align*}
p(t+dt)&=\frac{p(t)\odot (1+ v\ dt)}{p(t)\cdot (1+ v\ dt)}\\
&=p(t)\odot \frac{1+ v\ dt}{1 + \bar{v}\ dt}\\
&= p(t)\odot (1+v\ dt) \odot (1-\bar{v}\ dt)\\
&= p(t)\odot (1+v\ dt-\bar{v}\ dt)\\
\implies dp/dt&= p(t)\odot(v-\bar{v})
\end{align*}
This is the differential form of Bayes's theorem, and is the replicator equation from
evolution.

In the RL/entropic mirror ascent case, we have:
\begin{align*}
p(t+dt) &=\frac{p(t)\odot \exp\left(\nabla f(p(t))\ dt\right)}{p(t)\cdot \exp\left(\nabla f(p(t))\ dt\right)}\\
&= p(t)\odot (1 +\nabla f(p(t))\ dt-p(t)\cdot \nabla f(p(t))\ dt)\\
dp/dt&= p(t)\odot (\nabla f(p)-p\cdot \nabla f(p))
\end{align*}
Which is the same replicator equation, with the gradient of the reward $\nabla f(p(t))$ as
the ``evidence rate'' and the expected reward $p(t)\cdot \nabla f(p(t))$ as the ``expected
evidence rate''.

But notice: this $\nabla f(p)-p\cdot \nabla f(p)$ is \textbf{Advantage}, the difference
between the $Q$ and $V$ values!

Without ``rivalry'' (the constraint that probabilities would add up to 1), you would just
have $dp/dt= p(t)\odot \nabla f(p)$ (equivalent to the equation from the previous
section---the inverse Fisher information is $\diag(p)$ so left-multiplying by it is
equivalent to doing $p(t)\odot$). \textbf{\emph{Advantage} arises from rivalry}, i.e.\ the
constraint that these probabilities must add up to 1 (indeed, using advantage instead of the
$Q$-value is only compulsory for tabular softmax parameterized policies).

\subsection{What I'm confused about: dynamics}\label{agency:sec:dynamics-confusion}

Finally, the thing I am actually confused about:

You know, a big problem in life is that people like expressing everything in terms of
equilibrium (in economics, game theory,
\href{https://yuxi.ml/essays/equilibrium-thermoeconomics}{entropostatics aka
thermodynamics}) with little regard for the \emph{dynamics} that leads to the equilibrium.

(This is closely related to how we just model agents as rational (i.e.\ imagine that the
utility function can just be arg-maxed) instead of modeling bounded rationality which has
computational costs (and you can't just minus them from the objective either, because you
don't know what those costs are) and is fundamentally dynamic because you can't instantly
optimize something but that computational time is itself a cost\ldots)

Entropic mirror ascent seems like a very promising model of dynamics. Because evolution (the
most fundamental learning algorithm) actually follows it, and it is naturally a dynamical
Bayesian inference. And I have some work suggesting that economies can naturally be thought
of as doing it too.

But on the other hand \ldots\ I can't imagine how dynamical thermodynamics could have
anything to do with this? The simplest actually dynamical example in thermodynamics is
Newton's law of cooling, and that actually happens to be \textbf{gradient ascent on
entropy}, not continuous Bayesian inference/entropic mirror ascent.

Modeling thermodynamic dynamics fundamentally seems to have something to do with thinking
like ``Ok, I am not completely uncertain about this because I know where the system was 1
second ago; that is somehow a soft constraint'', i.e.\ a generalized notion of ``fraction''.
And I don't even know how gradient ascent on entropy comes out of this, maybe Newton's law
of cooling is just a coincidence?

\section{Setup}\label{agency:sec:setup}
% Source: main article, Section 1.

Here is how we will define an ``economy''.

\begin{definition}[economy]\label{agency:def:economy}
There is a vector space $\Qspace$ in which quantity vectors live (so its dimensionality is
the number of unique goods). There is a directed graph, where every node $i$ is a
``factory'' labelled with production function $f_i:\Qspace\to\Qspace$ and every edge $ij$ is
labelled by a quantity vector $q_{ij}\in\Qspace$. The graph is subject to physical
constraints:
$$f_i(x_i)=y_i$$
where $x_i=\sum_k q_{ki}$ and $y_i=\sum_j q_{ij}$. Furthermore some nodes, called
``consumers'', have a utility function $U_i(x_i,y_i)$.
\end{definition}

We may imagine a global welfare objective $W = \sum_i U_i(x_i, y_i)$ (WLOG all the weights
are 1).

\textbf{U.S.O.W.} We will usually think of $(V,E)$ as a DAG and $f_i, U_i$ are continuous
and differentiable; utilities have no explicit dependence on flows or shares. Cyclic graphs
are certainly \emph{interesting}, but they are hard in general.

(\textbf{But what about factories choosing what to produce? Shouldn't $f_{i}(x_i)$ be a set
and $y_i$ be chosen from this set?} For now we will treat that as a set of every possible
$f_i$, and the choice of what to produce amounts to routing to the right $f_i$. Maybe this
is bad from a computational POV or whatever; alternatively we may consider the $f_i$'s
themselves as trainable sub-agents with some internal parameters.)

\subsection{General price recursion}\label{agency:sec:price-recursion}

Introduce Lagrange multipliers $\lambda_i$ for the production constraints:
$$\mathcal L = \sum_i U_i(x_i,y_i) -\sum_i \lambda_i\cdot (y_i-f_i(x_i))$$
Differentiate with respect to quantity vectors $q_{i\to j}$, we get the recursion:
\begin{equation}
\lambda_i=\nabla_{y_i}U_i+\nabla_{x_j}U_j+J_{f_j}(x_j)^\top\lambda_j\tag{1.1}\label{agency:eq:lambda-recursion}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ the price of output from $i$ equals the direct marginal utility/cost at $i$, plus the
direct marginal utility at $j$, plus the downstream production value obtained by sending the
good through $j$. The term $J_{f_j}(x_j)^\top\lambda_j$ is the backpropagation operation.

However, this does not directly let us perform backpropagation on the market graph, since
$q_{i\to j}$ are not free variables: they must satisfy the constraint
$\sum_j q_{i\to j}=y_i$. So instead parameterize quantity flows using routing shares:
$$q_{i\to j}=s_{ij}\odot y_i$$
$$s_{ij,g}\geq 0,\qquad \sum_j s_{ij,g}=1$$

The forward computation is (where $\odot$ indicates the elementwise product, and $r_i$ is an
exogenous resource endowment if any):
$$x_i = r_i+\sum_h s_{hi}\odot y_h$$
$$y_i=f_i(x_i)$$

Defining adjoints $a_i=\frac{\partial W}{\partial x_i}$ and
$b_i=\frac{\partial W}{\partial y_i}$, $c_{ij}=\frac{\partial W}{\partial s_{ij}}$ and
differentiating:
\begin{equation}
a_i=\nabla_{x_i}U_i+J_{f_i}(x_i)^\top b_i\tag{1.2}\label{agency:eq:adjoint-a}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
b_i=\nabla_{y_i}U_i+\sum_j s_{ij}\odot a_j\tag{1.3}\label{agency:eq:adjoint-b}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
c_{ij} = y_i\odot a_j\tag{1.4}\label{agency:eq:share-gradient}
\end{equation}
Now, these shadow price propagation equations themselves are just \emph{true}: not dependent
on any particular dynamics/optimization algorithm. They are highly \emph{suggestive} of a
backprop-like dynamics, and the fact that these prices actually are so important in
real-world economic decision-making suggests that this suggestiveness is right.

You can transform the shares into logit space to do normal backpropagation without simplex
constraints, or you can directly do optimization on a simplex, e.g.\ via entropic mirror
ascent which gives the update rule (with geometric learning rate $\eta$) as in
Section~\ref{agency:sec:cbi}. Regardless, the optimization rule is always: ``move outputs
towards buyers with higher marginal value $a_j$''. At equilibrium, all active destinations
of the same good from the same source have equal marginal value, i.e.\ for active edges
($q_{i\to j,g}>0$, $q_{i\to k,g}>0$):
$$a_{j,g}=a_{k,g}$$

\section{Markets are MDPs; advantage = rivalrous goods}\label{agency:sec:mdp}
% Source: main article, Section 2.

Here is a nice interpretation of our economic model. Imagine there is only \emph{one} unit
good, i.e.\ a particle in the whole market. If the production functions are identity, then
its movement through the market is governed by policy $s_{ij,g}$, collecting rewards
$\nabla U$ along the way.

Production makes the particle ``branch'': at node $i$ a unit of good becomes a bundle
$J_{f_i} \cdot (\text{unit})$. We may regard the Jacobian $J_{f_i}$ as a ``matrix-valued
discount factor''. We also have a Bellman recursion:
\begin{equation}
\bar a_{i} := \sum_j s_{ij}\odot a_{j}\tag{2.1}\label{agency:eq:vbar}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
a_j = \underbrace{\nabla_{x_j} U_j+ J_{f_j}^\top\nabla_{y_j} U_j}_{\text{reward}} + J_{f_j}^\top \bar{a_i} \tag{2.2}\label{agency:eq:bellman}
\end{equation}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
RL & market \\
\midrule
state                         & ``one unit of good $g$ sits at node $i$''             \\
action                        & ``ship it to $j$''                                    \\
policy $\pi(j \mid i)$        & routing share $s_{ij,g}$                              \\
reward                        & marginal utility $\nabla U$ collected along the way   \\
action value $Q(i,j)$         & destination price $a_{j,g}$                           \\
state value $V(i)$            & average received price $\bar a_{i,g}$                 \\
advantage $A(i,j)$            & price premium $a_{j,g} - \bar a_{i,g}$                \\
visitation measure $d^\pi(i)$ & quantity present $y_{i,g}$                            \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Optimizing this market (computing the $s_{ij}$s) is then equivalent to solving this MDP. In
fact, we have a ``Policy Gradient Theorem'' (the $c_{ij}$
equation~\eqref{agency:eq:share-gradient} from earlier):
$$c_{ij} := \frac{\partial W}{\partial s_{ij}} = y_i\odot a_j$$

Or parameterizing with logits $s_{ij,g} = \exp(\theta_{ij,g}) / \sum_k \exp(\theta_{ik,g})$:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial W}{\partial \theta_{ij,g}}
  \;=\; s_{ij,g}\, y_{i,g} \left( a_{j,g} - \bar a_{i,g} \right)
  \;=\; q_{i\to j, g} \left( a_{j,g} - \bar a_{i,g} \right)\tag{2.3}\label{agency:eq:logit-gradient}
\end{equation}

\begin{proof}
Chain rule through the softmax Jacobian
$\partial s_{ik}/\partial \theta_{ij} = s_{ik}(\delta_{kj} - s_{ij})$ applied
to~\eqref{agency:eq:share-gradient}.
\end{proof}

In fact even the interpretation of $J_{f_i}^\top$ as a ``matrix-valued discount factor'' has
a natural interpretation. Generalizing $\gamma <1$, we have in a cyclic economy the
Hawkins--Simon condition $\rho(S^\top J_f) < 1$ where $S$ is the full matrix of $s_{ij}$'s
(for a particular good) and $\rho$ is the spectral radius (the largest absolute eigenvalue),
which tells us that a cyclic economy cannot be a perpetual motion machine.

(Well actually, it kinda can be a perpetual motion machine, if the dynamics of the cyclic
economy represent actual time rather than just convergence to an equilibrium. But tbh I
would rather represent that unrolled, as an infinite graph with nodes stretching out into
the future.)

(In the ``wide market RL'' I introduced in my paper
\href{https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.05828}{Market-based RL}, I don't think of the MDP itself as
a market, but the transition probabilities are determined by an additional Garrabrant-like
program market of traders and the network is more ``fundamentally dynamic'' rather than
graph-like. Something in-between---dynamic like program markets but efficient like
graphs---like a graph that forms new connections or performs search of some sort when shadow
prices exceed some friction-induced background transaction cost, seems like a good model for
describing real markets, Knightian uncertainty etc.)

\section{Chain markets are MLPs}\label{agency:sec:mlp}
% Source: main article, Section 3; remarks folded in from THEORY.md Section 5.

\begin{theorem}[chain = MLP]\label{agency:thm:chainmlp}
Let the economy be a directed chain $0 \to 1 \to \cdots \to L$ with resource $r$ injected at
node $0$, production functions $f_\ell$, and a single consumer with utility $u$ at node $L$.
Then all shares are trivially equal to 1, and:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The forward pass is the feedforward computation $h_{\ell+1} = f_\ell(h_\ell)$,
  $h_0 = r$, with welfare $W = u(h_L)$: \textbf{goods bundles are activation vectors} (one
  neuron per good; a node/factory is a whole layer).
  \item The market's shadow prices are the backprop gradients:
  $a_\ell = \partial W / \partial h_\ell = J_{f_\ell}^\top \cdots J_{f_{L-1}}^\top \nabla u$.
  \item \textbf{Internal parameterization.} If $f_\ell$ has parameters $w_\ell$, then by
  Lemma~\ref{agency:lem:alignment}, gradient ascent by each firm on its own profit at posted
  prices is identical to backprop/SGD on the network's weights:
  $\nabla_{w_\ell}(\text{profit}_\ell) = \nabla_{w_\ell} W$.
  \item Conversely, every MLP whose activations are nonnegative (i.e.\ with ReLU or similar
  activation functions) arises this way, with \textbf{bias vectors = exogenous endowments}
  $r_\ell$ injected at layer $\ell$ and the objective function as the terminal consumer's
  utility.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
1--2 are the forward/backward recursions of Section~\ref{agency:sec:setup} with a single
out-edge; 3 is Lemma~\ref{agency:lem:alignment}; 4 is construction.
\end{proof}

Theorem~\ref{agency:thm:chainmlp} completely formalizes the ``economies = backprop/neural
networks'' intuition that people have been groping at. Note that economies are more
\emph{general} than classical MLPs: they are neural networks with \emph{routing} (which is
kind of ``transposed static attention'': in markets, the goods are scarce while in
transformers, the receiver-side attention budget is scarce).

% The following remarks are lifted from THEORY.md Section 5 (whose Theorem 5 restates
% Theorem \ref{agency:thm:chainmlp} and is therefore not repeated in
% Section \ref{agency:sec:theory}).
\begin{remark}\label{agency:rem:chainmlp}
(i) The nonnegativity caveat is real: goods live in $\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$, so
ReLU-family/positive nets embed directly; signed activations require signed goods or
good-pairs (long/short). (ii) The converse direction shows the market class is
\emph{strictly larger}: a general market is an MLP \textbf{plus trainable conserved
routing}---in the language of Section~\ref{agency:sec:theory-attention}, a feedforward net
interleaved with transposed-attention layers. (iii) An economy is ``deep'' in the same sense
a network is: chains give compositional depth; routing gives width and credit-assigned
allocation.
\end{remark}

\section{Continuous Bayesian inference as a dynamics for markets}\label{agency:sec:cbi}
% Source: main article, Section 4.

In Section~\ref{agency:sec:hyperstition}
(\href{https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/z7DBBn9RaDyKJRidg/reward-is-hyperstitional-information}{Reward
as hyperstitional information}), I conveyed that entropic mirror ascent (aka continuous-time
Bayesian inference) is the natural dynamics for probabilities. Rewards and such are just an
``evidence rate''.

It seems very suggestive that our routing shares are literally just probabilities (of ``a
particle of good being sent to some factory''). Indeed, we can let the dynamics of our
market be ``entropic mirror ascent on total welfare'' i.e.\ update the shares each time step
as (indices $i,j,g$ are implicit):
$$s'=\argmax_{s'} \{\eta\langle \partial W/\partial s, s'\rangle - \KL(s'\|s)\}$$
From~\eqref{agency:eq:share-gradient} we know that the share gradient equals the revenue
rate $\partial W/\partial s = ya =: c$ thus the solution to this is (read
Section~\ref{agency:sec:hyperstition} if this isn't immediately clear):
\begin{equation}
s' \propto s \exp{(\eta c)}\tag{4.1}\label{agency:eq:mwu}
\end{equation}

Differentiate with respect to time to get the usual replicator flow/continuous-time Bayesian
inference:
\begin{equation}
\dot s = s\left( c - \bar c \right) \tag{4.2}\label{agency:eq:replicator}
\end{equation}

So: \textbf{the market (under entropic mirror ascent) is a Bayesian learner}, with each
agent doing ``hyperstitious'' learning. I.e.\ each agent $i$ has a belief distribution
$s_{i,-}$ on the question ``where will I send an infinitesimal quantity to?'' and the
infinitesimal evidence it receives for each hypothesis $j$ is exponentiated revenue ratio
$\exp{(\eta c_{ij})}/Z$.

\begin{thesisclaim}
Revenues are rewards are information (log-likelihoods).
\end{thesisclaim}

You can use this equivalence to transfer any result.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llp{4.2cm}p{5.2cm}@{}}
\toprule
domain & mass & fitness / evidence & equilibrium \\
\midrule
evolution & population shares      & reproductive fitness & fitness equalized (Fisher) \\
markets   & supply shares / wealth & prices, revenue      & law of one price, no arbitrage \\
inference & posterior probability  & log-likelihood       & likelihoods equalized/posterior support \\
RL        & policy $\pi(j \mid i)$ & $Q$-values ($d^\pi(i)\,Q(i,j)$ visitation-weighted; advantage $A = Q - V$ as the relative form) & advantage vanishes on support, $\le 0$ off it---Bellman optimality/policy improvement stops; generally a greedy deterministic policy, or Boltzmann $\pi \propto e^{Q/\tau}$ under entropy regularization \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Much like Fisher's fundamental theorem of natural selection we have the ``fundamental
theorem of welfare dynamics'': fix the technologies $f_i$ and utilities $U_i$ and let all
shares evolve simultaneously by~\eqref{agency:eq:replicator}. Then
\begin{equation}
\dot{W}
  = \sum_{i \in V} \sum_{g \in G} y_{i,g}^2\;
        \Var_{j \sim s_{i\cdot,g}}\!\left( a_{j,g} \right) \tag{4.3}\label{agency:eq:ftwd}
\end{equation}

\begin{proof}
$\dot W = \sum_{i,j,g} \frac{\partial W}{\partial s_{ij,g}} \dot s_{ij,g}$ (since the
partials are total derivatives). Substituting~\eqref{agency:eq:share-gradient}
and~\eqref{agency:eq:replicator}, each $(i,g)$ block contributes
$y_{i,g}^2 \sum_j s_{ij,g} a_{j,g}(a_{j,g} - \bar a_{i,g}) = y_{i,g}^2 (\mathbb{E}[a^2] - \bar a^2)$.
\end{proof}

\section{Markets rationalize reward and identity}\label{agency:sec:rationalize}
% Source: main article, Section 5.

I started my MATS work with the following observation:

\begin{quote}
Utility functions and hyperstitional ``identities'' (in the sense of predictive
processing---i.e.\ you do what you predict you'll do) are the same thing.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Both are ``arbitrary'': the choice of utility function, nor the co-ordinated choice
  of equilibrium (or whatever) are determined by existing rules of rationality.
  \item Purely predictive agents (i.e.\ \textbf{non-embedded} agents, \emph{not}
  Predict-o-matic) do not have identities; their beliefs are completely specified by Bayes
  (in the limit of all information being supplied). [I mean sure you could say that some
  priors may not allow convergence to the same thing, or debate what it means to take the
  limit of ``all information being supplied'', or talk about boundedly rational agents
  having ``globally inconsistent'' beliefs, but this seems like a qualitatively different
  thing.]
\end{enumerate}
So ideally, we would like to have a theory where internal sub-agent co-ordination is
equivalent to a choice of utility function. More precisely: the theory should connect this
to \emph{reward} (which ultimately ``selects'' or trains the identity), and build a
satisfying picture that explains how that exogenous reward itself fits into a broader
predictive-processing-only picture.
\end{quote}

Or more simply: rational behaviour for a \emph{purely predictive}/non-embedded agent is
completely specified by Bayes's theorem, but for any agent that performs \emph{actions} in
the world/whose beliefs affect the world, there is an arbitrary thing called the ``utility
function''. The utility function is shaped by reward, but then reward is arbitrary.

I continued:

\begin{quote}
The picture I expect right now is vaguely something like:
\begin{itemize}
  \item ``the universe as a whole'' (or closed systems more generally) do not have
  identities/utility functions, but individual sub-agents do
  \item Identities are the result of the universe hierarchically enforcing its will on its
  sub-agents via reward/selection. [But the question of exactly how actions i.e.\ things
  causally determined by agent beliefs, emerge, should be clarified].
  \item Reward has to do with constraints in statistical mechanics
\end{itemize}
This intuition is largely based on Point 2, and also the suggestive connection between
evolutionary fitness functions and Bayesian evidence
(\href{https://arxiv.org/abs/0911.1763}{Harper 2009, The Replicator Equation as an Inference
Dynamic}), which supports the idea that evolutionary selection is somehow the universe
``inserting its information'' into an agent.
\end{quote}

Now observe how markets have two processes going on simultaneously:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Agents learn the shares $s_{ij}$}, e.g.\ through continuous-time Bayesian
  inference, with prices (or rather revenue) as the ``information'' or reward supplied.
  \item \textbf{Price recursion} i.e.\
  equations~\eqref{agency:eq:lambda-recursion}--\eqref{agency:eq:share-gradient} or
  equivalently \eqref{agency:eq:vbar}--\eqref{agency:eq:bellman} which follow a
  Bellman/backprop-like rule.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{quote}
\emph{FAQ: Wait, how can price recursion be equivalent to both Bellman and backprop?}

Backprop/reverse-mode autodiff on \emph{any} computation graph produces a ``Bellman-shaped''
recursion, i.e.\ value of a node = sum of its consumer nodes' values transformed by
$J_{f_i}^\top$.

What makes the market price recursion actual Bellman (with matrix-valued discount factors
$J_{f_i}^\top$) rather than merely ``Bellman-shaped'' is the simplex constraint which lets
the routing shares be interpreted as probabilities.
\end{quote}

In other words:

\begin{thesisclaim}
The economy is exactly the ``super-agent'' that generates reward to its sub-agents, and
price recursion is the ``rational theory of reward''.
\end{thesisclaim}

In Section~\ref{agency:sec:hyperstition}
(\href{https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/z7DBBn9RaDyKJRidg/reward-is-hyperstitional-information}{Reward
as hyperstitional information}), reward was entirely an underdetermined hyperstition.
Markets (equations \eqref{agency:eq:vbar}--\eqref{agency:eq:bellman}) tell you how to
determine it.

\subsection{Setup and notation}\label{agency:sec:theory-setup}

An \textbf{economy} is a finite directed graph $(V, E)$ over a goods space
$G = \{1, \dots, n_g\}$. Each node $i$ has a production function
$f_i : \mathbb{R}^{n_g}_{\ge 0} \to \mathbb{R}^{n_g}_{\ge 0}$ and optionally a utility
$U_i(x_i, y_i)$. Routing is parameterized by shares: for each source $i$ and good $g$, the
vector $s_{i \cdot, g} = (s_{ij,g})_{j : (i,j) \in E}$ lies on the simplex
$\simplex_i = \{ s \ge 0, \sum_j s_{ij,g} = 1 \}$, and
$$q_{i \to j} = s_{ij} \odot y_i, \qquad
  x_j = r_j + \sum_i q_{i \to j}, \qquad
  y_i = f_i(x_i).$$

The planner objective is welfare $W = \sum_i \alpha_i U_i(x_i, y_i)$ (take $\alpha_i = 1$
w.l.o.g.). Throughout, \textbf{adjoints} are total derivatives along the graph:
$$a_i = \frac{\partial W}{\partial x_i} \quad (\text{input value / shadow price at } i),
\qquad
  b_i = \frac{\partial W}{\partial y_i} \quad (\text{output value at } i),$$
computed by the backward recursion (SPEC \S4.3)
$$b_i = \nabla_{y_i} U_i + \sum_j s_{ij} \odot a_j, \qquad
  a_i = \nabla_{x_i} U_i + J_{f_i}(x_i)^\top b_i.$$

Define the \textbf{average price received} by producer $i$:
$$\bar a_{i,g} \;=\; \sum_j s_{ij,g}\, a_{j,g} \;=\; b_{i,g} - \partial_{y_{i,g}} U_i .$$

\begin{assumption}[standing assumptions]\label{agency:ass:standing}
$(V,E)$ is a DAG and $f_i, U_i$ are $C^1$; utilities have no explicit dependence on flows or
shares. All statements extend to cyclic graphs whenever the fixed point
$x = r + S^\top f(x)$ is locally unique and differentiable in $s$ (then $a$ is the implicit
adjoint of SPEC \S6.2 and every identity below holds verbatim with these total derivatives).
\end{assumption}

The basic share-gradient identity (SPEC \S4.4) is
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial W}{\partial s_{ij,g}} = y_{i,g}\, a_{j,g} \tag{0.1}\label{agency:eq:theory-share-gradient}
\end{equation}
---the marginal welfare of routing more of good $g$ from $i$ to $j$ is the volume shipped
times the shadow price at the destination.

\subsection{The advantage identity (softmax logits = policy gradient)}\label{agency:sec:theory-advantage}

Recall from RL: say we have an MDP with states like $i$, actions like $j$ and a
differentiable policy $\pi_\theta(j\mid i)$ parameterized by $\theta$. Then in general the
expected return is:
$$J(\theta)
= \sum_{i} d^\pi(i) V^\pi(i)
= \sum_{i} d^\pi(i) \sum_{j} \pi_\theta(j \mid i) Q^\pi(i, j)$$
where $d^\pi(s) = \lim_{t \to \infty} P(s_t = s \mid s_0, \pi_\theta)$ is the stationary
distribution of the Markov chain under $\pi$. The policy gradient theorem tells us that the
gradient of the expected return can be calculated by just differentiating the policy:
$$\nabla_\theta J \;=\; \sum_i d^\pi(i) \sum_j \nabla_\theta\, \pi_\theta(j \mid i)\; Q^\pi(i,j)$$
While we are used to thinking of $\theta$ as like neural net parameters, this can also be
applied to tabular RL agents, i.e.\ $\theta$ simply being the matrix $\pi(j|i)=s_{ij}$.

Parameterize $s_{ij,g} = \exp(\theta_{ij,g}) / \sum_k \exp(\theta_{ik,g})$.

\begin{theorem}[advantage identity; \verifiedmark]\label{agency:thm:advantage}
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial W}{\partial \theta_{ij,g}}
  \;=\; s_{ij,g}\, y_{i,g} \left( a_{j,g} - \bar a_{i,g} \right)
  \;=\; q_{i\to j, g} \left( a_{j,g} - \bar a_{i,g} \right). \tag{1.1}\label{agency:eq:theory-advantage}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Chain rule through the softmax Jacobian
$\partial s_{ik}/\partial \theta_{ij} = s_{ik}(\delta_{kj} - s_{ij})$ applied
to~\eqref{agency:eq:theory-share-gradient}.
\end{proof}

\textbf{Interpretation.} Gradient ascent on routing logits raises the share sent to buyer
$j$ if and only if $j$'s shadow price exceeds the flow-weighted average price the producer
currently receives, at a rate proportional to current volume. This is \emph{``shift supply
toward buyers paying above your average price.''}

\begin{corollary}[law of one price]\label{agency:cor:onelaw}
At any stationary point of~\eqref{agency:eq:theory-advantage} with $q_{i\to j,g} > 0$ for
all $j$ in the support, $a_{j,g} = \bar a_{i,g}$ for every supported destination: all buyers
actually served pay the same shadow price, for every producer and every good,
simultaneously across the graph.
\end{corollary}

\subsubsection*{The reinforcement-learning reading, exactly}

The \textbf{policy gradient theorem} \citep{sutton2000}, in its canonical form, is stated
with $Q$-values and is parameterization-agnostic: for any differentiable policy
$\pi_\theta(j \mid i)$ in a Markov decision process, the gradient of the expected return $J$
is
\begin{equation}
\nabla_\theta J \;=\; \sum_i d^\pi(i) \sum_j \nabla_\theta\, \pi_\theta(j \mid i)\; Q^\pi(i,j), \tag{1.2}\label{agency:eq:theory-pgt}
\end{equation}
where $d^\pi$ is the state-visitation measure and $Q$ the action value---\textbf{no
advantage in sight}. The advantage form is the theorem plus two observations:

\textbf{(i) Baseline freedom} (a lemma, not part of the theorem): since
$\sum_j \pi(j \mid i) = 1$, differentiating gives $\sum_j \nabla_\theta \pi(j \mid i) = 0$,
so any action-independent baseline $b(i)$ may be subtracted from $Q$
in~\eqref{agency:eq:theory-pgt} without changing the sum. In RL practice one \emph{chooses}
$b = V$---this changes nothing in expectation but shrinks the variance of Monte Carlo
estimators of~\eqref{agency:eq:theory-pgt}.

\textbf{(ii) Softmax specialization} (mechanical---no choice involved): substitute the
\textbf{tabular softmax} parameterization---one free logit per state-action pair,
$\pi_\theta(j \mid i) = e^{\theta_{ij}} / \sum_k e^{\theta_{ik}}$, i.e.\ $\theta$ is a table
(a function of $i$ \emph{and} $j$, not a generic weight vector) and the softmax is taken
row-wise, over the actions of each state separately. This ``tabular'' case---no function
approximation, parameters = policy degrees of freedom moved to log-coordinates---is exactly
the routing-logit parameterization above: one $\theta_{ij,g}$ per (source, destination,
good). Since row $i$ is the only row containing $\theta_{ij}$, the derivative stays within
one state, and the softmax Jacobian
$\partial \pi(k \mid i)/\partial \theta_{ij} = \pi(k \mid i)\big(\delta_{kj} - \pi(j \mid i)\big)$
performs the baseline subtraction \emph{automatically}, with exactly $b = V$:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial J}{\partial \theta_{ij}} \;=\; d^\pi(i)\; \pi_\theta(j \mid i)\; A^\pi(i,j),
\qquad A^\pi(i,j) = Q^\pi(i,j) - V^\pi(i), \quad V^\pi(i) = \mathbb{E}_{j \sim \pi}[Q^\pi(i,j)]. \tag{1.3}\label{agency:eq:theory-advantage-pgt}
\end{equation}

The mechanism: raising one logit necessarily lowers every \emph{other} action's probability,
and the value lost through the others is precisely the $\pi$-weighted average of their
$Q$'s---that is where $V$ comes from. So ``theorem with $Q$'' and ``theorem with $A$'' are
the same statement; the advantage appears the moment the normalization constraint is
differentiated through.

The market has the same two-layer structure: the share-gradient
identity~\eqref{agency:eq:theory-share-gradient} is the $Q$-form---ambient partial
derivatives, gross values, no baseline---while Theorem~\ref{agency:thm:advantage} is the
softmax form~\eqref{agency:eq:theory-advantage-pgt}. Baseline freedom has an economic name
too: \textbf{num\'eraire freedom}. Any feasible reallocation satisfies
$\sum_j \dot s_{ij} = 0$, so
$\langle a + c \mathbf{1}, \dot s \rangle = \langle a, \dot s \rangle$: adding a constant to
every destination's price changes no routing decision---only price \emph{differences} act on
the simplex. RL's baseline invariance and economics' ``only relative prices matter'' are the
same fact.

\textbf{Theorem~\ref{agency:thm:advantage} is~\eqref{agency:eq:theory-advantage-pgt}}, term
for term, under the dictionary of Section~\ref{agency:sec:mdp}.

%% TODO(agency): trimmed from THEORY.md §1.2 here: the "How exact is this?" paragraph
%% (pass-through economies make the MDP reading literal; general production makes the
%% particle branch, with the backward recursion as a Bellman equation) and the
%% "Goods diffuse like probability mass; prices are its value function, unpacked"
%% paragraph — both are covered in substance by Section \ref{agency:sec:mdp}.

\textbf{Where the advantage comes from (it is rivalry).} The raw share
gradient~\eqref{agency:eq:theory-share-gradient},
$\partial W/\partial s_{ij,g} = y_{i,g}\, a_{j,g}$, is the $Q$-side: the \emph{gross} value
of routing to $j$, no baseline subtracted. The advantage appears the moment any update
respects the simplex: on $\sum_j s_{ij,g} = 1$ you cannot send more to $j$ without sending
less elsewhere, so only value \emph{relative to the counterfactual destination} can
matter---and every simplex-respecting geometry subtracts a baseline automatically. Euclidean
projection onto the tangent space $\{\sum_j v_j = 0\}$ subtracts the uniform average;
softmax logits and the Fisher--Rao geometry of Section~\ref{agency:sec:theory-replicator}
subtract the flow-weighted average $\bar a = V$. In all cases the baseline is the Lagrange
multiplier of the conservation constraint. So: \textbf{advantage = rivalry.} And the answer
to ``what is the rival resource in RL?'' is: \emph{probability itself.} A policy owns
exactly one unit of it per state; actions compete for probability mass the way buyers
compete for goods. (In attention, the same unit budget is each query's attention---see
Appendix~\ref{agency:sec:attention-scarce}.)

\textbf{Actor--critic = the market protocol.} In this dictionary the price system is a
\emph{critic}, trained by policy evaluation: the backward sweep is exact evaluation, and the
relaxed adjoint iteration $\lambda \leftarrow \nabla_x W + J_F^\top \lambda$ (SPEC \S7.2) is
iterative, bootstrapped evaluation---temporal-difference learning in all but name. The share
update is the \emph{actor}, moving on advantages. The decentralized protocol of
Section~\ref{agency:sec:theory-decentralization} is thus a two-timescale actor--critic:
quotes (critic) relax fast, routing (actor) moves slowly. The vanilla/natural distinction
maps too: gradient flow on logits = vanilla softmax policy gradient; replicator flow =
natural policy gradient \citep{kakade2001}---see the end of
Section~\ref{agency:sec:theory-replicator}.

\subsection{Replicator dynamics and the fundamental theorem}\label{agency:sec:theory-replicator}

Let $G_{ij,g} = y_{i,g} a_{j,g}$ denote the share
gradient~\eqref{agency:eq:theory-share-gradient} and
$\bar G_{i,g} = \sum_k s_{ik,g} G_{ik,g} = y_{i,g} \bar a_{i,g}$. Consider the
\textbf{replicator flow}, run simultaneously on every simplex $(i, g)$:
\begin{equation}
\dot s_{ij,g} \;=\; s_{ij,g}\left( G_{ij,g} - \bar G_{i,g} \right)
  \;=\; s_{ij,g}\, y_{i,g} \left( a_{j,g} - \bar a_{i,g} \right). \tag{2.1}\label{agency:eq:theory-replicator}
\end{equation}
This is the \textbf{replicator equation} of evolutionary game theory \citep{taylor1978}:
population = producer $i$'s supply of good $g$; strategies = the destinations $j$;
\textbf{fitness of a strategy = the revenue rate $y_{i,g} a_{j,g}$ at its shadow price}.

\subsubsection*{Three derivations of one flow}

Fix a producer-good pair and drop indices: shares $s \in \simplex$, payoff vector $g$
(components $G_{ij,g}$ over destinations), average payoff $\bar g = \langle s, g \rangle$.

\textbf{(a) Small-step limit of multiplicative reweighting.} The discrete market rule
``multiply each destination's share by its exponentiated revenue, then renormalize'' is
multiplicative weights,
$$s_j^+ = \frac{s_j\, e^{\eta g_j}}{Z}, \qquad Z = \sum_k s_k\, e^{\eta g_k},$$
where $Z$ is simply the normalizer returning the update to the simplex---economically: ``I
ship all of what I have,'' so only \emph{relative} payoffs can matter, and indeed adding a
constant to every $g_k$ cancels in $Z$. Expanding in $\eta$:
$$s_j^+ = s_j\, \frac{1 + \eta g_j + O(\eta^2)}{1 + \eta \bar g + O(\eta^2)} = s_j + \eta\, s_j (g_j - \bar g) + O(\eta^2),$$
so $(s^+ - s)/\eta \to s_j(g_j - \bar g)$: \textbf{the replicator flow is the
small-learning-rate limit of the library's \code{mirror\_ascent}} (SPEC \S5.1). This is the
precise content of ``continuous-time limit of entropic mirror ascent.''

\textbf{(b) Steepest ascent when step size is measured in KL (natural gradient).}
``Steepest ascent'' is meaningless until one fixes what counts as a small step. On a simplex
the statistically meaningful metric is Fisher--Rao, $\|v\|_s^2 = \sum_j v_j^2 / s_j$, which
is nothing but infinitesimal KL divergence:
$\KL(s + v \,\|\, s) = \tfrac12 \|v\|_s^2 + O(\|v\|^3)$. Maximize $\langle g, v \rangle$
over tangent vectors ($\sum_j v_j = 0$) of fixed Fisher--Rao length: Lagrange stationarity
gives $v_j \propto s_j (g_j - \mu)$, and the tangency constraint forces $\mu = \bar g$. The
steepest-ascent direction under the KL yardstick \textbf{is} $s(g - \bar g)$: replicator =
Fisher--Rao natural gradient (Shahshahani metric). This is the same Fisher information
metric as in statistics---the geometric reason replicator and Bayes coincide in
Section~\ref{agency:sec:theory-inference}.

\textbf{(c) Dual averaging: keep a ledger, allocate by softmax.} Let a producer keep a
cumulative additive ledger of raw payoffs, $\theta(t) = \theta_0 + \int_0^t g\, d\tau$, and
always post $s(t) = \softmax(\theta(t))$. Differentiating: $\dot s_j = s_j (g_j - \bar g)$,
the replicator again. Note carefully \emph{what is deposited}: the raw payoff $g$, not the
damped logit gradient $\partial W / \partial \theta = s(g - \bar g)$. That one-word
difference is the entire gap between mirror ascent and logit SGD (see the comparison at the
end of this subsection).

\begin{theorem}[fundamental theorem of welfare dynamics; \verifiedmark]\label{agency:thm:ftwd}
Fix the technologies $f_i$ and utilities $U_i$ and let all shares evolve simultaneously
by~\eqref{agency:eq:theory-replicator}. Then
\begin{equation}
\frac{dW}{dt}
  \;=\; \sum_{i \in V} \sum_{g \in G} y_{i,g}^2\;
        \Var_{j \sim s_{i\cdot,g}}\!\left( a_{j,g} \right)
  \;\ge\; 0. \tag{2.2}\label{agency:eq:theory-ftwd}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$\dot W = \sum_{i,j,g} \frac{\partial W}{\partial s_{ij,g}} \dot s_{ij,g}$ (the partials are
total derivatives, so this is exact for the coupled system).
Substituting~\eqref{agency:eq:theory-share-gradient}
and~\eqref{agency:eq:theory-replicator}, each $(i,g)$ block contributes
$y_{i,g}^2 \sum_j s_{ij,g} a_{j,g}(a_{j,g} - \bar a_{i,g}) = y_{i,g}^2 (\mathbb{E}[a^2] - \bar a^2)$.
\end{proof}

This is \textbf{R.A. Fisher's fundamental theorem of natural selection}
\citep{fisher1930}---\emph{the rate of increase of mean fitness equals the variance in
fitness}---transplanted verbatim, with fitness = price:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Welfare grows at exactly the flow-weighted variance of prices across destinations.
Price dispersion---unexploited arbitrage---is the genetic variance that fuels welfare
growth; equilibrium is the exhaustion of variance, i.e.\ the law of one price.}
\end{quote}

Under plain logit gradient flow the rate is instead
$\dot W = \sum_{i,j,g} q_{ij,g}^2 (a_{j,g} - \bar a_{i,g})^2$---a second moment rather than
a variance, with the same zero set.

\begin{corollary}[convergence]\label{agency:cor:convergence}
$W$ is nondecreasing and (if bounded above on the compact product of simplices, with $x(s)$
continuous) converges; every $\omega$-limit point satisfies the law of one price on its
support, for every producer and good. No claim of convergence to the \emph{global} optimum
is made without concavity---with nonconvex technologies the flow can terminate at local
welfare optima, exactly as neural-network training can.
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[complementary slackness = evolutionary stability]\label{agency:cor:slackness}
A stationary point with $s_{ij,g} = 0$ for some $j$ is stable against ``invasion'' of that
edge only if $a_{j,g} \le \bar a_{i,g}$: every unserved buyer offers weakly less than the
producer's going rate. Stability of boundary equilibria under replicator dynamics is
precisely KKT complementary slackness; an evolutionarily stable routing is a
\textbf{no-arbitrage} allocation.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[multi-layer structure; answer to ``is this just one node?'']\label{agency:rem:multilayer}
Theorem~\ref{agency:thm:ftwd} is \emph{not} a single-population statement: it holds with
every producer-good pair $(i,g)$ running its own replicator concurrently, in an arbitrary
DAG (verified numerically on a 3-layer, 2-good economy). The right frame is
\textbf{multi-population replicator dynamics with endogenous fitness}: population $(i,g)$'s
fitness landscape $a_{j,g}$ is not exogenous but is itself recomputed by the pricing
recursion as all other populations move---prices are the mean-field coupling the
populations. Because all populations share the single potential $W$, the coupled system is a
\textbf{potential game}, and~\eqref{agency:eq:theory-ftwd} says the potential increases at
the total variance summed over populations. Interior deep equilibria satisfy the law of one
price at \emph{every layer simultaneously}, which is exactly the first-order condition
$\nabla_\theta W = 0$ of the deep network.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection*{One equilibrium, three flows (mirror ascent vs.\ logit ascent)}

Three ascent dynamics recur in this chapter and in the library. They share their stationary
points (law of one price on supports,
Corollaries~\ref{agency:cor:convergence}--\ref{agency:cor:slackness}) and the Lyapunov
function $W$, but they are \textbf{different trajectories}:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{2.9cm}p{2.4cm}p{2.6cm}p{2.6cm}p{3.2cm}@{}}
\toprule
 & update & share-space flow & RL name & character \\
\midrule
\textbf{D1} mirror ascent / MWU (\code{mirror\_ascent}) & $s \leftarrow s\, e^{\eta G} / Z$ & $\to$ replicator as $\eta \to 0$ & natural policy gradient (discrete) & market-canonical; each step a Bayes update; wealth compounding \\
\textbf{D2} replicator flow & \eqref{agency:eq:theory-replicator} & $\dot s = s(G - \bar G)$ & natural policy gradient flow & Fisher--Rao steepest ascent; $\dot W$ = variance \eqref{agency:eq:theory-ftwd} \\
\textbf{D3} logit gradient flow (\code{softmax\_logits}) & $\dot\theta = \partial W / \partial\theta$ & $\dot s_j = s_j(u_j - \langle s, u \rangle)$, $u_k = s_k(G_k - \bar G)$ & vanilla softmax policy gradient & autodiff-native; speed $\propto s^2$ near vertices, so it stalls at near-deterministic routings \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

D1 and D2 are the same object at small $\eta$ (derivation (a)); their ledger integrates the
\emph{raw} payoff $G$ (derivation (c)). D3's ledger integrates the softmax-damped signal
$s(G - \bar G)$ instead---one extra factor of the Fisher information---which is exactly the
gap between vanilla and natural policy gradient \citep{kakade2001};
\citet{agarwal2021} quantify it: natural PG enjoys dimension-free convergence rates, while
vanilla softmax PG can take exponentially long to escape a nearly-deterministic corner
\citep{li2021}.

\textbf{So, to the obvious question---``do the replicator dynamics arise from the natural
decentralized dynamics, or is that the logit ascent?'':} the dynamics with the clean
decentralized market reading (Section~\ref{agency:sec:theory-decentralization}:
multiplicative revenue reweighting) and the dynamics with the replicator/Bayes/variance
readings are \textbf{the same one, D1/D2}. D3 is a same-equilibria, different-clock cousin:
informationally just as local (its update uses only $q$ and $a - \bar a$), convenient under
autodiff/Adam (SPEC \S5.3), but with no compounding-wealth or Bayes interpretation and worse
behavior near the simplex boundary.

\subsection{Reverse attention: the market layer is the adjoint of an attention layer}\label{agency:sec:theory-attention}

Fix one good and collect the shares into a row-stochastic matrix
$S \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}_{\ge 0}$, $S \mathbf{1} = \mathbf{1}$ (row $i$ = how sender
$i$ splits over destinations). Compare with a \emph{static} attention layer with
row-stochastic weights $A$ ($\alpha_{i\cdot} \in \simplex$ = how receiver $i$ mixes over
sources).

Two linear operators are in play for the same stochastic matrix $P$:
$$\text{averaging} \quad \mathcal{K}_P : v \mapsto P v,
  \qquad
  \text{partition} \quad \mathcal{K}_P^* : y \mapsto P^\top y.$$

\begin{theorem}[reverse attention]\label{agency:thm:reverse-attention}
Let $P$ be row-stochastic. Then:

\textbf{(a) (Adjoint pair.)} $\langle P^\top y, v \rangle = \langle y, P v \rangle$: the
routing operator is the adjoint of the attention operator.

\textbf{(b) (Forward/backward swap.)} Since reverse-mode differentiation of a linear map is
its adjoint:
\begin{itemize}
  \item market forward pass $x = S^\top y$ \textbf{is} the attention \emph{backward} pass
  (gradients-to-values $\bar v = A^\top \bar o$), and
  \item market backward pass $\bar y_i = \sum_j s_{ij} a_j = (S a)_i = \bar a_i$ \textbf{is}
  the attention \emph{forward} pass ($o = A v$).
\end{itemize}
With $A = S$, each layer's forward pass is the other's backward pass.

\textbf{(c) (Conservation $\leftrightarrow$ copying.)} $P \mathbf{1} = \mathbf{1}$ (the
averaging side \emph{copies}: constant inputs pass through unchanged, and the same value
$v_j$ is delivered undiminished to every receiver) holds iff
$\mathbf{1}^\top P^\top y = \mathbf{1}^\top y$ (the partition side \emph{conserves}: total
mass is invariant). One condition, two readings.

\textbf{(d) (Markov duality.)} $P$ is a Markov kernel. The partition operator $P^\top$ is
its \textbf{Perron--Frobenius (pushforward) operator acting on measures}; the averaging
operator $P$ is its \textbf{Koopman (expectation) operator acting on functions}. Goods are
measures; prices are functions on states. The backward price relation
$\bar a_i = \mathbb{E}_{j \sim s_{i\cdot}}[a_j]$ says \textbf{prices are a martingale along
the goods flow}, and the law of one price (Corollary~\ref{agency:cor:onelaw}) says that at
equilibrium prices are a.s.\ constant along each producer's routing distribution.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(a) is $\langle P^\top y, v\rangle = y^\top P v$. (b) follows from (a) plus the definition
of a vector-Jacobian product. (c): both statements are $P\mathbf 1 = \mathbf 1$ read against
the two pairings. (d) is the standard measure/function duality for Markov kernels.
\end{proof}

\textbf{The dictionary.}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{4.2cm}p{4.6cm}p{4.6cm}@{}}
\toprule
 & attention & market routing \\
\midrule
simplex sits on & incoming edges of each \emph{receiver} & outgoing edges of each \emph{sender} \\
forward pass & convex combination (Koopman) & conserved partition (Perron--Frobenius) \\
what flows forward & information---\textbf{non-rival}, copied & goods---\textbf{rival}, conserved \\
what flows backward & credit/gradient---conserved, partitioned & prices---non-rival, copied \\
weights come from & current activations ($q^\top k$): \emph{dynamic} & trainable logits $\theta$: \emph{static} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

So ``reverse attention'' is exactly right, in the strong sense that the two layers are
mutually adjoint: \textbf{a market is attention run through the mirror, with goods in the
role of gradients and prices in the role of activations. The adjoint swaps rival and
non-rival.} NNs move copyable information forward and conserved credit backward; markets
move conserved goods forward and copyable price-information backward. No appeal to any
nonstandard architecture is needed---the statement is intrinsic linear algebra plus the
definition of reverse-mode AD.

\emph{(A self-contained exposition of attention itself---the soft-lookup picture, what
queries/keys/values are, why softmax and $\sqrt{d}$, multi-head---written to be read
alongside this section, is given in Appendix~\ref{agency:sec:attention}.)}

\begin{remark}[production interleaves]\label{agency:rem:production-interleaves}
A full market layer is $x \mapsto f(x) \mapsto S^\top f(x)$; its backward pass is
$a = J_f^\top (S\, a_{\text{next}})$. Production enters only through the Jacobian
transpose---the ``exchange rate'' of the price martingale---and does not disturb the duality
of the routing step.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[dynamic attention = price-responsive markets]\label{agency:rem:dynamic-attention}
Transformer attention computes its weights from the current state. The market analogue is
routing shares that respond to current bids/prices---which is precisely the decentralized
simulator's \code{SoftmaxClearing} ($s_{ij} \propto \exp(\beta\,\text{bid}_{ij})$):
\emph{softmax clearing is a literal attention layer whose queries are bids.} The planner
(static logits) and the simulator (bid-responsive clearing) sit at the static/dynamic
attention poles.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Decentralization: backprop + mirror ascent \emph{is} a market protocol}\label{agency:sec:theory-decentralization}

The planner algorithm (forward solve $\to$ adjoint solve $\to$ mirror step) looks
centralized. This section states the precise sense in which it is not: every quantity it
computes is a \textbf{node-local} computation on messages from graph neighbors, and every
update coincides with a myopically selfish response to posted prices.

\subsubsection*{First-order incentive alignment}

Give each node $i$ the objective (``utility-inclusive profit at posted prices $p$''):
$$J_i \;=\; \underbrace{\sum_j p_j \cdot (s_{ij} \odot y_i)}_{\text{revenue}}
      \;-\; \underbrace{\sum_k \pi_i \cdot q_{k \to i}}_{\text{input bill}}
      \;+\; U_i(x_i, y_i),$$
where $p_j$ are prices posted by $i$'s buyers and $\pi_i$ is the price vector $i$ posts for
its inputs. Let node $i$'s \textbf{own controls} be its output shares $s_{i\cdot}$ and any
internal technology parameters $w_i$ of $f_i$.

\begin{lemma}[alignment]\label{agency:lem:alignment}
If posted prices equal adjoints, $p_j = a_j$ for all buyers $j$ of $i$, then for every
control $u \in \{s_{i\cdot}, w_i\}$:
$$\nabla_u J_i \;=\; \nabla_u W .$$
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For shares:
$\partial J_i / \partial s_{ij,g} = y_{i,g} p_{j,g} = y_{i,g} a_{j,g} = \partial W / \partial s_{ij,g}$
by~\eqref{agency:eq:theory-share-gradient}; the input bill does not depend on $i$'s controls
(received quantities are set by upstream shares). For technology:
$\nabla_{w} J_i = (\partial y_i/\partial w)^\top (\nabla_{y_i} U_i + \sum_j s_{ij} \odot p_j) = (\partial y_i / \partial w)^\top b_i = \nabla_w W$.
\end{proof}

At shadow prices, \textbf{selfish first-order incentives coincide exactly with the welfare
gradient}---for routing decisions \emph{and} for technology investment. This is a
differential form of the first welfare theorem, and it is the economic content of backprop:
\emph{the price system is what aligns local greed with global credit assignment.}

\subsubsection*{The protocol}

One round, on a DAG (all steps use only own state + neighbor messages):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Ship} (forward sweep): each node receives $q_{k\to i}$, produces
  $y_i = f_i(x_i)$, ships $q_{i\to j} = s_{ij} \odot y_i$.
  \item \textbf{Quote} (backward sweep): each node, having observed the prices $p_j$ posted
  by its buyers, posts for its own suppliers the bid
  \begin{equation}
  \pi_i \;=\; \nabla_{x_i} U_i \;+\; J_{f_i}(x_i)^\top
     \Big( \nabla_{y_i} U_i + \sum\nolimits_j s_{ij} \odot p_j \Big)
     \tag{4.1}\label{agency:eq:theory-quote}
  \end{equation}
  ---\emph{``I bid my marginal value: what one more unit of input is worth to me, given what
  my buyers pay me.''} Pure consumers post $\pi_i = \nabla_{x_i} U_i$.
  \item \textbf{Reroute}: each producer updates each good's shares by multiplicative weights
  on its own revenue:
  $s_{ij,g} \leftarrow s_{ij,g} \exp(\eta\, y_{i,g}\, p_{j,g}) / Z_{i,g}$, where
  $Z_{i,g} = \sum_k s_{ik,g} \exp(\eta\, y_{i,g}\, p_{k,g})$ is the normalizer returning the
  shares to the simplex (derivation (a) above)---``I ship all of what I have; only relative
  track records matter.''
\end{enumerate}

\begin{theorem}[decentralized equivalence]\label{agency:thm:decentralized}
On a DAG, the price fixed point of step 2 is exactly the adjoint system: $\pi_i = a_i$ for
every node, reached in one reverse sweep (depth-many asynchronous rounds if updates are
unordered). Given these prices, step 3 is identical to the planner's \code{mirror\_ascent}
update. Hence the trajectory of the protocol equals the trajectory of
\code{SharesOptimizer(update\_rule="mirror\_ascent")}, and by
Lemma~\ref{agency:lem:alignment} each agent is at every step ascending its \emph{own}
objective. On cyclic graphs, repeatedly iterating step 2 is precisely the relaxed adjoint
iteration of SPEC \S7.2---\emph{asynchronous price negotiation converging to mutually
consistent quotes.}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\eqref{agency:eq:theory-quote} with $p_j = a_j$ is literally the backward recursion of
Section~\ref{agency:sec:theory-setup}; uniqueness on a DAG is induction on reverse
topological order. The update coincidence is definitional (SPEC \S5.1).
\end{proof}

\textbf{What is derived and what is posited.} The \emph{equations} of the backward pass are
not an ML import: \eqref{agency:eq:theory-quote} is ``value inputs at marginal revenue
product,'' the classical competitive pricing condition, and it is computable locally---this
is the Hayekian decentralization of backprop. What \emph{is} behavioral: (i)
\textbf{price-taking truthfulness}---nodes bid their true marginal value rather than shading
bids (strategic bidding, market power, and thin markets are open questions here; note that
persistent underbidding is punished in the repeated dynamics because suppliers' replicator
responses divert goods to better payers); (ii) \textbf{myopia}---agents follow instantaneous
gradients rather than planning ahead; (iii) \textbf{no budgets}---bids
in~\eqref{agency:eq:theory-quote} are not wealth-constrained, i.e.\ this is the
Negishi-weighted planner, not a Fisher market. Budgets/wealth are exactly where the
decentralized simulator departs from the planner and where Eisenberg--Gale/
proportional-response theory enters \citep{wu2007,birnbaum2011}
(see Section~\ref{agency:sec:theory-inference}).

\begin{remark}[on ``who controls what'']\label{agency:rem:controls}
In this protocol a node controls its \emph{output shares} and posts \emph{input bids},
matching the natural reading of the shares parameterization (``a producer decides where its
product goes; a buyer signals what inputs are worth''). Bids are per node-good, not per
edge---marginal value is a property of the good at the node, so a single $\pi_{i,g}$ is
quoted to all suppliers (edge-specific bids only become meaningful with transport costs or
price discrimination, both future extensions in SPEC). Note the input bid is \emph{not}
chosen to maximize $J_i$ myopically (the myopic optimum is to bid zero); it is the truthful
demand signal whose fixed point supports the welfare optimum. An honest game-theoretic
treatment of bidding is the main open problem of this section.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[toward perfect competition]\label{agency:rem:perfect-competition}
The honest resolution of the price-taking postulate is a large-market limit in which
price-taking becomes a theorem rather than an assumption. The classical benchmarks: core
convergence under replication \citep{debreu1963} and economies with a continuum of traders
\citep{aumann1964}, where every individual agent has measure zero and \emph{cannot} move
prices. The natural program for this library: replace the finite graph by a measure space of
nodes (a \textbf{graphon economy}); let each producer control an infinitesimal share of
every market it participates in; then bids aggregate by a law of large numbers, prices
become deterministic functionals of the mean field, individual bid-shading has vanishing
effect (the classical $1/N$ Cournot-to-Walras rate), and the coupled replicator system of
Section~\ref{agency:sec:theory-replicator} becomes a replicator \emph{continuity
equation}---a mean-field game whose stationary points are Walrasian. Open questions: (i)
does the $N \to \infty$ limit of the honest \emph{strategic} bidding game converge to the
protocol above (Cournot--Walras convergence, transplanted onto backprop)? (ii) what is the
graphon limit of the network itself---do flows and prices converge to continuum fields
(goods as densities, prices as their dual fields), giving a PDE limit of the economy?
\end{remark}

\subsection{Markets as inference: replicator = Bayes}\label{agency:sec:theory-inference}

\subsubsection*{Continuous-time Bayes}

Let $p_t$ be a posterior over a finite hypothesis set $H$ under i.i.d.\ evidence with
log-likelihood rate $\ell_t(h)$. Bayes' rule $p_{t+dt}(h) \propto p_t(h) e^{\ell_t(h)\,dt}$
gives, in the limit,
$$\dot p_t(h) \;=\; p_t(h) \left( \ell_t(h) - \mathbb{E}_{p_t}[\ell_t] \right),$$
which \textbf{is the replicator equation with fitness = log-likelihood}
\citep{harper2009,shalizi2009}. Equivalently: Bayes = multiplicative weights on
log-evidence; the posterior is $\softmax(\log p_0 + \int \ell)$.

\subsubsection*{The market as a Bayesian learner}

Compare with the log-space form of the market flow
(Section~\ref{agency:sec:theory-replicator}):
$s_{i\cdot,g}(t) = \softmax\!\big(\theta_0 + \int_0^t y_{i,g}\, a_{\cdot,g}\, d\tau\big)$.

\begin{proposition}[markets do Bayesian updating]\label{agency:prop:bayes}
Under mirror ascent / replicator dynamics, producer $i$'s routing distribution for good $g$
evolves as the Bayesian posterior over the hypothesis set
$\{ \text{``} j \text{ is the best use of my good''} \}_j$, with prior $s_{i\cdot,g}(0)$ and
accumulated log-evidence $\int \eta\, y_{i,g}\, a_{j,g}\, dt$---i.e.\ \textbf{revenue
streams are log-likelihoods; the price premium $a_j - \bar a_i$ is the instantaneous
log-likelihood ratio; the law of one price is the statement that at equilibrium no
destination carries further evidence against another.}
\end{proposition}

This is a formal identification, not an analogy: the two ODEs coincide under fitness
$\leftrightarrow$ log-likelihood. Its classical instance is \textbf{Kelly betting}
\citep{kelly1956} (a horse-race gambler's Kelly-optimal wealth shares track the Bayesian
posterior; wealth grows at the information rate), and its market-selection form is
\citet{blume2006}: in repeated markets, wealth flows to agents whose beliefs are closest in
KL divergence to the truth---the market as a whole implements Bayesian model averaging with
wealth as probability. Cover's universal portfolio \citep{cover1991} is the same
mathematics.

\subsubsection*{What the three faces mean}

One dynamic, three readings (see the table in Section~\ref{agency:sec:cbi}). The
fundamental theorem~\eqref{agency:eq:theory-ftwd} is the same statement in all three:
\emph{variance of fitness = rate of adaptation; price dispersion = rate of welfare growth;
expected log-likelihood-ratio (information gain) = rate of learning.} The philosophical
upshot is that Hayek's ``markets aggregate information'' can be read literally in this
model: \textbf{prices are the economy's log-likelihoods, the allocation is its posterior,
and equilibration is inference.} Conversely, since the same dynamic trains neural networks
(Sections~\ref{agency:sec:theory-advantage}--\ref{agency:sec:theory-replicator}),
\emph{learning, evolution, arbitrage, and inference are one process expressed in four state
spaces}---in this library, that sentence is an executable theorem rather than a slogan.

\section{The \texttt{marketgrad} library}\label{agency:sec:library}
% Source: marketgrad/README.md and marketgrad/SPEC.md (Sections 1 and 21).

\code{marketgrad} is a NumPy-first library for market-like optimization on production
graphs. It provides graph primitives, differentiable production and utility functions,
shares-based welfare optimization, cyclic fixed-point and adjoint solvers, simple
decentralized market dynamics, primal-dual optimization scaffolding, diagnostics,
visualizations, examples, and tests. \draft{The theory of this chapter is maintained
alongside the library: identities are checked numerically against the implementation
(Section~\ref{agency:sec:theory-ledger}).}

The core abstraction is a directed graph of productive, consumptive, and resource nodes.
Goods flow along edges. Nodes transform input bundles into output bundles via production
functions. Some nodes receive utility from input and/or output bundles. The library supports
several algorithmic interpretations of such systems:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Shares-based welfare optimization.} A centralized, physically feasible,
  backpropagation-like optimizer where edge quantities are parameterized by routing shares.
  \item \textbf{Decentralized market-like dynamics.} A more realistic simulation of agents,
  bids, prices, wealth, budgets, ownership, sales, and production over time.
  \item \textbf{Primal-dual constrained optimization.} A general
  Lagrangian/augmented-Lagrangian/ADMM-style solver that can handle constraints not easily
  captured by shares, possibly allowing temporary physical infeasibility.
\end{enumerate}

The central conceptual bridge is:
\begin{center}
\code{backprop adjoints $\approx$ shadow prices $\approx$ Lagrange multipliers}
\end{center}
and:
\begin{center}
\code{gradient ascent over feasible routing decisions $\approx$ reallocating goods toward
higher marginal value}
\end{center}

The library supports both acyclic and cyclic production graphs. In acyclic graphs,
quantities can be computed by a topological forward pass and prices by a reverse backward
pass. In cyclic graphs, quantities are defined by fixed-point equations and prices by
implicit adjoint equations, as in deep equilibrium models.

The primary clean algorithm is shares-based optimization:
\begin{quote}
forward pass: compute feasible quantities \\
backward pass: compute adjoint prices \\
update shares: route goods toward higher marginal value
\end{quote}
For acyclic graphs, this is ordinary forward/backward propagation. For cyclic graphs, this
becomes a fixed-point solve for quantities and an implicit adjoint solve for prices. The
default share update is entropic mirror ascent,
\code{s\_new $\propto$ s\_old * exp(learning\_rate * marginal\_value)}; projected gradient
and softmax-logit optimization are also supported, but as alternative update geometries. The
decentralized market simulator is the realistic economic dynamics layer (agents bid, trade,
produce, earn, spend, and update prices locally), and the primal-dual solver is the advanced
constrained-optimization backend (quantities and multipliers co-evolve, possibly with
temporary constraint violations).

The library's purpose is to make the analogy between markets and backpropagation precise,
programmable, visualizable, and experimentally testable. JAX and PyTorch backends are
optional (\code{marketgrad[jax]}, \code{marketgrad[torch]}); without either extra, the
default NumPy backend and finite-difference autodiff remain unchanged.

\section{What a transformer's router actually does}\label{agency:sec:attention}
% Source: marketgrad-theory/ATTENTION.md (lifted in full).

\begin{draftblock}
This appendix reproduces the companion exposition \texttt{ATTENTION.md} to
Section~\ref{agency:sec:theory-attention}. It is self-contained---no prior comfort with
transformers assumed. The running translation is the one proved there (attention and market
routing are adjoint), so every concept is introduced twice: once in network language, once
in market language.
\end{draftblock}

\subsection{One sentence}\label{agency:sec:attention-onesentence}

\textbf{Attention is a layer whose connection weights are not parameters but are computed
from the data at run time---the network draws its own wiring diagram for each input---with
the wiring chosen by content matching, and kept differentiable by replacing ``retrieve the
best match'' with ``average, weighted by match quality.''}

Everything else---queries, keys, values, softmax, $\sqrt{d}$, heads---is bookkeeping in
service of that sentence.

\subsection{The problem: wiring that depends on content}\label{agency:sec:attention-wiring}

An ordinary layer $h' = \sigma(Wh)$ has its communication pattern frozen into $W$: entry
$W_{uv}$ says how strongly slot $v$ influences slot $u$, the same for every input, forever
after training. Call this \emph{position-addressed} communication. It is the right tool when
``who should listen to whom'' is a fixed fact about the architecture.

For a \emph{set} of tokens it is the wrong tool twice over. In ``the cat that the dog chased
was black,'' the token \emph{was} needs to consult \emph{cat}---not because \emph{cat} sits
at position 2, but because of what \emph{cat} is; in the next sentence the subject will sit
somewhere else. So (i) the useful wiring differs per example, and (ii) a set has no
canonical slots for position-addressed weights to even refer to.

The fix is radical: \textbf{promote the weights from parameters to activations.} Parameters
are slow memory, written by gradient descent over a lifetime of training; activations are
fast memory, computed fresh each forward pass. Attention is a layer that \emph{computes} a
stochastic weight matrix from the current tokens and immediately uses it to move
information:
$$\underbrace{W_{\text{fast}} = \softmax(\text{match scores})}_{\text{decide the wiring}},
\qquad \underbrace{o = W_{\text{fast}} \cdot (\text{messages})}_{\text{use it}}.$$

This divide already exists inside \code{marketgrad}: the planner's routing shares are
\emph{slow} weights (trained logits, fixed at ``inference''), while the simulator's
\code{SoftmaxClearing} recomputes shares from current bids at every tick---\emph{fast}
weights. Static vs.\ dynamic attention is exactly planner vs.\ clearing
(Remark~\ref{agency:rem:dynamic-attention}).

\subsection{Soft lookup: the differentiable dictionary}\label{agency:sec:attention-lookup}

Start from an actual dictionary lookup, \code{d[query] $\to$ value}. A hard lookup scores
each stored key against the query, takes the argmax, and returns that entry's value. Two
problems: argmax is not differentiable, and a wrong-but-close key returns nothing at all.

Soften it: score every key, turn scores into weights with softmax, return the weighted
average of \emph{all} values:
$$\alpha_j = \softmax_j\!\big(\langle q, k_j \rangle\big), \qquad o = \sum_j \alpha_j\, v_j.$$

A three-key micro-example: scores $(2.0,\ 0.5,\ -1.0)$ give weights $(0.79,\ 0.18,\ 0.04)$
---mostly the best match, with a whisper of the runner-up, and a gradient flowing into
\emph{every} score. Attention is \textbf{content-addressable memory with a soft address}.

There is a deeper way to say this. The formula
$$o(q) = \frac{\sum_j e^{\langle q, k_j \rangle}\, v_j}{\sum_j e^{\langle q, k_j \rangle}}$$
is \emph{Nadaraya--Watson kernel regression} \citep{nadaraya1964,watson1964}: the textbook
nonparametric estimator of a conditional expectation $\mathbb{E}[v \mid q]$, with kernel
$K(q,k) = e^{\langle q,k \rangle}$. Attention answers the question \emph{``what, on average,
is said by things that resonate with what I'm looking for?''} A transformer layer is every
token simultaneously running kernel regression against all the others---with the kernel
itself learned.

\subsection{Query, key, value: three roles, one token}\label{agency:sec:attention-qkv}

Each token's vector $h$ is linearly projected three ways:
$$q = W_Q h \ \ (\text{what I seek}), \qquad k = W_K h \ \ (\text{what I advertise}),
\qquad v = W_V h \ \ (\text{what I deliver}).$$

Why not use $h$ itself for all three? Because the roles have different \emph{types}, and
conflating them forces bad compromises:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The features by which \emph{cat} should be \textbf{findable} (subject-noun-ish,
  animate) are not the features \emph{cat} should \textbf{deliver} once found (its actual
  semantics). Key $\ne$ value: the label on the filing cabinet is not the file.
  \item Relevance is \textbf{directed}: ``\emph{was} should consult \emph{cat}'' does not
  imply ``\emph{cat} should consult \emph{was}.'' The score
  $\langle q_i, k_j \rangle = h_i^\top W_Q^\top W_K\, h_j$ is a learned \emph{asymmetric}
  bilinear form---a relevance metric, usually low-rank ($d_k \ll d$): compatibility is
  judged in a cheap subspace while the cargo travels in a richer one.
\end{itemize}

The market reading makes the three roles vivid: the \textbf{query is a bid sheet} (a buyer's
declaration of what inputs it needs), the \textbf{key is a listing} (a supplier's
advertisement of what it offers), and the \textbf{value is the shipment} (what actually
arrives, which is neither the ad nor the want). $QK^\top$ is the compatibility matrix of a
marketplace; each softmax row is one buyer spreading its unit demand across the suppliers
whose listings match its bid sheet. An attention layer is one clearing round of a
procurement market for information.

\subsection{What is scarce: the two conservation laws}\label{agency:sec:attention-scarce}

Here is the question that unlocks the \code{marketgrad} connection: \emph{in a transformer,
what is conserved?}

Not the values. The same $v_j$ is delivered \textbf{at full strength to every token that
wants it}---information is non-rival; reading a message does not use it up. So the
merchandise is free to copy, and the softmax cannot be conserving \emph{it}.

What the softmax conserves is on the receiver's side: each query's weights sum to one.
\textbf{Every token has exactly one unit of attention to spend per layer.} That is the
scarce resource---the layer is named after the only thing in it that is rationed. An
attention layer is a \emph{demand-side} market: buyers with unit budgets, shopping among
freely replicable suppliers.

Market routing is the mirror image: a \emph{supply-side} market. Each seller has one unit of
(rival, conserved) goods to split over buyers; the simplex sits on the \emph{outgoing} edges
instead of the incoming ones. Transpose one and you get the other---that is
Theorem~\ref{agency:thm:reverse-attention}.

And the transformer's rival, conserved flow does exist---it runs \textbf{backward}. In
backprop through $o_i = \sum_j \alpha_{ij} v_j$, receiver $i$'s incoming gradient
$\bar o_i$ is \emph{partitioned} over the sources it attended, in proportion $\alpha_{ij}$,
with $\sum_j \alpha_{ij} = 1$; summing over receivers, total gradient mass is exactly
conserved. Credit is rationed the way goods are. So:
\begin{itemize}
  \item transformer forward = market for \textbf{information} (non-rival, copied);
  transformer backward = market for \textbf{credit} (rival, conserved);
  \item \code{marketgrad} forward = market for \textbf{goods} (rival, conserved);
  \code{marketgrad} backward = market for \textbf{prices} (non-rival---every supplier reads
  the same posted price).
\end{itemize}

Training a transformer clears a credit economy backward through every layer; running an
economy trains a transformer transposed. Each is the other read through the adjoint.

\subsection{Softmax, temperature, $\sqrt{d}$: keeping the auction competitive}\label{agency:sec:attention-softmax}

\textbf{Why softmax specifically?} It is the unique way to turn scores into weights that (i)
yields a genuine probability distribution---needed for the ``weighted average / conditional
expectation'' reading; (ii) is shift-invariant---only score \emph{differences} matter, and
there is no absolute zero of relevance; and (iii) carries a temperature dial $\beta$
interpolating between the two things a lookup could mean:
$$\beta \to 0: \ \text{uniform averaging (attention off)}, \qquad
\beta \to \infty: \ \text{hard argmax retrieval (winner-take-all)}.$$

Gibbs' characterization makes (iii) principled: $\softmax(\beta x)$ is the maximum-entropy
distribution with the prescribed expected score---the \emph{least committal} soft choice
consistent with favoring high scores. And
$\tfrac{1}{\beta} \log \sum_j e^{\beta x_j} \to \max_j x_j$: softmax is the gradient of the
smooth max.

\textbf{Why divide by $\sqrt{d_k}$?} Dot products of $d_k$-dimensional vectors with $O(1)$
coordinates have magnitude $\sim \sqrt{d_k}$. Feed raw scores into softmax at, say,
$d_k = 64$ (std $\approx 8$) and the distribution is already nearly one-hot \emph{at
initialization}: the auction opens as a monopoly, gradients through the softmax die, and no
price discovery ever happens. Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ sets the opening temperature to
$O(1)$---a competitive auction with live gradients. The same dial exists in
\code{marketgrad} twice: \code{SoftmaxClearing(beta=...)}, and the planner's entropy
regularization $\tau$, where $\tau \to 0$ gives sharp law-of-one-price allocations and
$\tau > 0$ gives smooth gravity-model trade. Choosing $\sqrt{d_k}$ is choosing where on the
uniform-to-argmax dial training begins.

\subsection{Multi-head attention = multiple goods}\label{agency:sec:attention-heads}

One attention pattern per layer means one scalar currency of relevance. But tokens relate in
several ways \emph{at once}---syntactic role, coreference, topic, position---and a single
softmax must smear them together. Multi-head attention runs $H$ independent attention
markets in parallel low-dimensional subspaces (each with its own $W_Q, W_K, W_V$) and
concatenates the $H$ retrieved bundles.

In \code{marketgrad} language, \textbf{heads are goods}. Vector goods give each good its own
routing simplex per seller; multi-head gives each relevance-type its own attention simplex
per buyer. This makes the correspondence of Section~\ref{agency:sec:theory-attention}
dimensionally exact---$H$ heads $\leftrightarrow$ $H$ goods, head by head, good by
good---and it explains the design the same way economics would: you cannot clear steel and
labor through one price, and you cannot route syntax and topic through one attention
pattern. \draft{(Attention with directly trained static weights---the network-side twin of
static routing logits---is the Synthesizer architecture of \citealp{tay2020}.)}

\subsection{The stack: trade, then production}\label{agency:sec:attention-stack}

\emph{Self}-attention just means the set attends to itself: every token is buyer and seller
in the same round. A transformer block then alternates two moves:
$$\text{attention: tokens exchange information} \ \longrightarrow\ \text{MLP: each token
processes what it received, alone}.$$

A \code{marketgrad} economy alternates the same two moves: routing (goods are exchanged)
$\to$ production functions (each node transforms its inputs, alone). \textbf{The MLP block
is the production function.} Depth is re-trading: aggregate, transform, trade again. A
transformer is a deep economy for information run on the demand side; a \code{marketgrad}
economy is a deep transformer for goods run on the supply side. (Cross-attention---a decoder
querying an encoder---is import trade between two economies; positional encodings just stamp
``where'' into content, so that even location must compete for attention as information.)

\subsection{The punchline}\label{agency:sec:attention-punchline}

Strip attention to its skeleton and it is: a row-stochastic matrix, applied to a stack of
vectors. Strip market routing to its skeleton and it is: the \emph{transpose} of a
row-stochastic matrix, applied to a stack of vectors. Reverse-mode differentiation of a
linear map is its adjoint---so each layer's forward pass is the other's backward pass, and
the pair (copying constants, conserving mass) is one condition $P\mathbf{1} = \mathbf{1}$
read in the two dual pairings. Formal statement and proof:
Theorem~\ref{agency:thm:reverse-attention}.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Attention distributes a budget of belief over freely copyable messages. A market
distributes a budget of stuff over freely copyable price signals. They are the same
operator, read from opposite sides of the duality between measures and functions.}
\end{quote}
