AI alignment refers to the problem of ensuring AI agents pursue their principals' goals. It is natural to expect that \emph{economics} provides the natural framework to express and study this problem: the problem of reward specification (``alignment to what?'') is just value elicitation, and the problem of incentive alignment is handled in economics via the market mechanism or in general by the field of mechanism design. Yet, only a handful of papers have directly expressed this view, e.g. [Bergemann (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.01595) and [Conitzer (2024)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.5555/3692070.3692441). 

More broadly, there are general intuitive arguments to expect a close formal relationship between the fields of machine learning and economics. Philosophers and psychologists have pondered multi-agent models of the mind \citep{minskySocietyMind1988}; moreover, many have long noted intuitive analogies between economies and neural networks with backpropagation and sought a more precise mathematical equivalence: \href{https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/brhWPoNsBN7za3xjs/competitive-markets-as-distributed-backprop}{John Wentworth}, Aanjaneya Kumar and Anders Sandberg at the \href{https://ai.objectives.institute/projects}{AI Objectives institute}, and the ancient Neural Bucket Brigade (\href{https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/1988/hash/82aa4b0af34c2313a562076992e50aa3-Abstract.html}{Davis}, \href{https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/09540098908915650}{Schmidhuber}). The analogy is attractive and suggestive:


\begin{enumerate}
  \item Economies are intelligent agents built out of sub-agents much less capable than
  themselves\footnote{See e.g. Hayek on the role of markets in aggregating information \citep{hayekEconomicsKnowledge1937} -- or the famous parable ``I, Pencil'' \citep{leonardereadPencilMyFamily1958}: ``... no one person, no matter how smart, could create from scratch a small, everyday pencil [yet the market makes over a billion of them each year] ...''. There is also some empirical work on emergent intelligent behaviour in markets comprised of zero-intelligence traders \citep{godeAllocativeEfficiencyMarkets1993, schwartzHowMuchIrrationality2008, jamalSimpleAgentsIntelligent2015}}; in some sense, they ``aggregate'' the intelligence or capacities of their individual participants.
  \item There is work suggesting that markets are a model for boundedly rational decision-making, e.g. the \emph{Boundedly Rational Inductive Agent} (BRIA) \citep{oesterheldTheoryBoundedInductive2023} and \emph{Algorithmic Bayesian Epistemology} \citep{neymanAlgorithmicBayesianEpistemology2024} (intuitively, the efficient market hypothesis may be imagined as expressing that markets are efficient conditional on the \emph{logical information} available: and an inexploitability condition with respect to the traders present in the market is exactly how we may formalize this intuition). ``Market dynamics'' is famously under-studied because again it is
  fundamentally computational and boundedly-rational rather than simply caring about the existence of an equilibrium. % TODO: cite stuff. E.g. the Kamal Jain quote. You might find some references in my y0 report (PhD proposal), also mention the results (should also be cited in the proposal IIRC) showing that equilibirum computation is computationally inefficient.
  \item Markets have analogs of inconsistency (arbitrage) and
  \href{https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/xqxXrAohXSD3akYCg/meaningful-things-are-those-the-universe-possesses-a}{G\"odel's
  incompleteness theorem}: a prediction market is limited in its ability to efficiently
  price the question ``This question will resolve YES if this market gives less than 50\%
  probability to it''. (More specifically, a market must give zero subsidy to such a
  question: its subjective ``value for information'' on that question must be zero.)
  \item Prices are gradients: specifically: gradients on activations, not
  weights---they do not zero out at equilibrium. So at the very least, we know that both markets and neural networks involve local constrained optimization algorithms on a directed graph.
\end{enumerate}

Both markets and \emph{boundedly rational} agents are only ``rational given the available algorithmic information''. Markets have \emph{self-awareness}: they can contain prediction markets for the market's own properties, and can also self-improve, because the algorithmic functionality of markets is itself provided by goods (e.g. ``the labour supply of arbitrageurs'') whose supply can be adjusted and optimized. Abstraction is like \emph{economies of scale}; \emph{transaction costs} are like \emph{computational costs}. Markets are \emph{general intelligence}: they can be asked (paid) to accomplish \emph{any} task --- and they are intelligent about managing this generality, forming abstractions (factor goods) that will be useful for the distribution of tasks they expect to be asked to accomplish.

\section{Economics for agent foundations}

One promising approach to an "economic theory of agency" lies in the existing corpus of literature on what we will refer to as *Inexploitable Program Markets*. In traditional examples (classifiers, Baum... cite the whole spiel), we solve a Markov Decision Problem by auctioning off the "right to act on the world" at each time step to a computably-enumerable set of traders. This lets relatively simple programmatic traders act on the state sequentially, and each trader is incentivized to perform the action that maximize the reward earned plus the value of the state after its actions (i.e. creating a Bellman iteration). More recently, program markets have been applied as a model of logical uncertainty (Garrabrant) and bounded rationality (Oesterheld). 

Despite these promising models, program markets have not actually ... [lift sentence from paper]. Part of the reason has simply to do with the inherent inefficiency of "enumerating every trader", without any optimization over this search. But also, the program markets in the literature differ from real-world markets in a crucial way: real-world markets do not auction off the "entire world" to an agent at each stage. Instead, real markets have multiple *goods* which are separately auctioned without granting any agent a monopoly over the right to act entirely.

Over the course of this research, the following fundamental questions kept recurring, and organize the chapters that follow:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Can we design a reinforcement learning agent based on an internal market-based algorithm?} Would doing so have any advantages?
\item Some works have noted a suggestive market analogy in neural networks \citep{davisMappingClassifierSystems1988, schmidhuberLocalLearningAlgorithm1989} and in backpropagation \citep{wentworthCompetitiveMarketsDistributed2018}. \emph{Can we make this more precise, and use it to construct a market-based algorithm for supervised learning applications?}
\item Markets may be thought of as measuring devices for people's values and beliefs -- \emph{can we develop some ``AIs as producers, humans as consumers'' framework for giving human feedback to AIs, i.e. replacing naive RLHF?}
\item Prediction markets can easily give probabilities for verifiable or falsifiable (VF) events. But many questions we are interested in are not VF -- they exist only in the ``latent space''. \emph{Can we design scoring rules to incentivize usefully reporting probabilities on latent space variables?} The resulting economy may be seen as an AI that has learned some internal beliefs, and may be valuable for interpretability research \citep{christianoElicitingLatentKnowledge2021}.
\end{enumerate}
