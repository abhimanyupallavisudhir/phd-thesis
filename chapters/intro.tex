AI alignment refers to the problem of ensuring AI agents pursue their principals' goals. It is natural to expect that \emph{economics} provides the natural framework to express and study this problem: the problem of reward specification (``alignment to what?'') is just value elicitation, and the problem of incentive alignment is handled in economics via the market mechanism or in general by the field of mechanism design. Yet, only a handful of papers have directly expressed this view, e.g. \citet{bergemannMechanismDesignAlignment2026} and \citet{conitzerPositionSocialChoice2024}.

More broadly, there are general intuitive arguments to expect a close formal relationship between the fields of machine learning and economics. Philosophers and psychologists have pondered multi-agent models of the mind \citep{minskySocietyMind1988}; moreover, many have long noted intuitive analogies between economies and neural networks with backpropagation and sought a more precise mathematical equivalence: \citet{wentworthCompetitiveMarketsDistributed2018}, Aanjaneya Kumar and Anders Sandberg \citep{aiobjectivesinstituteProjects}, and the ancient Neural Bucket Brigade \citep{davisMappingClassifierSystems1988, schmidhuberLocalLearningAlgorithm1989}. The analogy is attractive and suggestive:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Economies are intelligent agents built out of sub-agents much less capable than
  themselves\footnote{See e.g. Hayek on the role of markets in aggregating information \citep{hayekEconomicsKnowledge1937} -- or the famous parable ``I, Pencil'' \citep{leonardereadPencilMyFamily1958}: ``... no one person, no matter how smart, could create from scratch a small, everyday pencil [yet the market makes over a billion of them each year] ...''. There is also some empirical work on emergent intelligent behaviour in markets comprised of zero-intelligence traders \citep{godeAllocativeEfficiencyMarkets1993, schwartzHowMuchIrrationality2008, jamalSimpleAgentsIntelligent2015}}; in some sense, they ``aggregate'' the intelligence or capacities of their individual participants.
  \item There is work suggesting that markets are a model for boundedly rational decision-making, e.g. the \emph{Boundedly Rational Inductive Agent} (BRIA) \citep{oesterheldTheoryBoundedInductive2023} and \emph{Algorithmic Bayesian Epistemology} \citep{neymanAlgorithmicBayesianEpistemology2024} (intuitively, the efficient market hypothesis may be imagined as expressing that markets are efficient conditional on the \emph{logical information} available: and an inexploitability condition with respect to the traders present in the market is exactly how we may formalize this intuition). ``Market dynamics'' is famously under-studied because again it is
  fundamentally computational and boundedly-rational rather than simply caring about the existence of an equilibrium. \draft{Indeed, computing equilibria is in general intractable: Nash equilibria are \textsf{PPAD}-complete \citep{daskalakisComplexityComputingNash2006, daskalakisComplexityComputingNash2009}, as are Arrow--Debreu equilibria even for additively separable utilities \citep{chenSettlingComplexityArrowDebreu2009}: thus Kamal Jain's quip as relayed in \citet{daskalakisComplexityComputingNash2009}, ``if your laptop cannot find it, then neither can the market''. The contrapositive is that a realistic model of markets is a model of bounded rationality \citep{papadimitriouComplexityBoundedRationality1994}: whatever the market does compute, it computes efficiently.}
  \item Markets have analogs of inconsistency (arbitrage) and G\"odel's incompleteness theorem \citep{pallavisudhirMeaningfulThings2022}: a prediction market is limited in its ability to efficiently
  price the question ``This question will resolve YES if this market gives less than 50\%
  probability to it''. (More specifically, a market must give zero subsidy to such a
  question: its subjective ``value for information'' on that question must be zero.)
  \item Prices are gradients: specifically, gradients on activations, not
  weights---they do not zero out at equilibrium. So at the very least, we know that both markets and neural networks involve local constrained optimization algorithms on a directed graph.
\end{enumerate}

Both markets and \emph{boundedly rational} agents are only ``rational given the available algorithmic information''. Markets have \emph{self-awareness}: they can contain prediction markets for the market's own properties, and can also self-improve, because the algorithmic functionality of markets is itself provided by goods (e.g. ``the labour supply of arbitrageurs'') whose supply can be adjusted and optimized. Abstraction is like \emph{economies of scale}; \emph{transaction costs} are like \emph{computational costs}. \section{Economics for agent foundations}\label{intro:sec:agency}

One promising approach to an ``economic theory of agency'' lies in the existing corpus of literature on what we will refer to as \emph{inexploitable program markets}. In traditional examples --- Holland's classifier systems and their ``bucket brigade'' \citep{hollandPropertiesBucketBrigade1985a}, Schmidhuber's extensions \citep{schmidhuberEvolutionaryPrinciplesSelfreferential1987, schmidhuberLocalLearningAlgorithm1989}, Baum's Hayek machine \citep{baumModelIntelligenceEconomy1999} and its successors for POMDPs \citep{kweeMarketBasedReinforcementLearning2001} and with Vickrey auctions \citep{changDecentralizedReinforcementLearning2020} --- we solve a Markov Decision Problem by auctioning off the ``right to act on the world'' at each time step to a computably-enumerable set of traders. This lets relatively simple programmatic traders act on the state sequentially, and each trader is incentivized to perform the action that maximizes the reward earned plus the value of the state after its actions (i.e. creating a Bellman iteration). More recently, program markets have been applied as a model of logical uncertainty \citep{garrabrantLogicalInduction2016} and bounded rationality \citep{oesterheldTheoryBoundedInductive2023}.

% "the performance of existing implementations ... unimpressive" is lifted
% from the market-based architectures paper's (earlier) abstract.
Despite these promising models, the performance of existing implementations of program market-based agents has been unimpressive. Part of the reason has simply to do with the inherent inefficiency of ``enumerating every trader'', without any optimization over this search. But also, the program markets in the literature differ from real-world markets in a crucial way: real-world markets do not auction off the ``entire world'' to an agent at each stage. Instead, real markets have multiple \emph{goods} which are separately auctioned, without granting any agent a monopoly over the right to act entirely.

\begin{researchquestion}\label{rq:goods}
Can we construct a program-market-based RL agent with ``modularity of state''---multiple separately traded goods, like in real-world goods markets---rather than a single-unit auction of the right to act?
\end{researchquestion}

% Lifted from the market-based architectures paper (abstract + contributions).
\begin{contribution}
In \cref{ch:market-rl} we present two general frameworks for market-based RL agents: (1) the ``deep market'' (\cref{mba:def:deep}), where a single good, the \emph{state}, is passed through a sequence of transacting agents, and (2) the ``wide market'' (\cref{mba:def:goods}), in which the state space itself is partitioned into several factors called \emph{goods}. 

The deep framework is not much of a departure from existing algorithms such as the Hayek machine, and can be applied to any Partially Observed Markov Decision Process; the wide framework is original to us, and is a generalization of the deep framework which better mirrors the success of real-world markets, allowing for greater specialization and parallelism.

We further argue that market-based algorithms have the potential to address current challenges in AI such as \emph{search}, \emph{dynamic scaling} and \emph{complete feedback}, and list some novel ways that market algorithms may be applied in conjunction with Large Language Models.
\end{contribution}

While program markets provide a model of a market-based boundedly-rational agent, they do not capture the intuitive equivalence that people have sought between markets and neural networks (we draw such an equivalence in \cref{mba:thm:nn}, but it is rather artificial and a stretch).

\begin{researchquestion}\label{rq:backprop}
Can we construct a natural model of market dynamics for which there is a precise mathematical equivalence to neural networks with backpropagation?
\end{researchquestion}

\begin{contribution}
In \cref{ch:agency} we define the \emph{graphical production economy} --- a directed graph of producers and consumers exchanging goods bundles, with routing shares deciding where each producer's output goes --- and demonstrate the following equivalences:
\begin{itemize}
  \item chain markets are equivalent to multi-layer perceptrons (\cref{agency:thm:chainmlp}): goods bundles are activation vectors, and the market's shadow prices are exactly the backpropagated gradients;
  \item production-less (pass-through) markets are equivalent to MDPs (\cref{agency:sec:mdp}): the price recursion is a Bellman recursion with matrix-valued discount factors, and the ``advantage'' of an action is a rivalrous good.
\end{itemize}
\end{contribution}

However, we would like a result that is stronger than a mere analogy. We would like to be able to say that markets enjoy a \emph{privileged} position amongst multi-agent models of agency, i.e. justify the following philosophical claim: \emph{An idealized market is a model of a ``perfectly rational'' multi-agent system in the same way Bayesian updating is a model of a perfectly rational predictive agent.}

\begin{researchquestion}\label{rq:reward}
\draft{Can the dynamics of a market be derived from a normative principle of rationality --- so that the market stands to \emph{reward} as Bayesian updating stands to \emph{evidence}, i.e. so that there is a \emph{rational theory of reward}?}
\end{researchquestion}

\begin{contribution}
\draft{In \cref{ch:agency} we show:
\begin{enumerate}
\item (\cref{agency:sec:hyperstition}) Entropic mirror ascent---the natural optimization dynamics in probability space---is continuous-time Bayesian inference (replicator dynamics), so a reward signal is exactly (hyperstitional) Bayesian evidence.
\item (\cref{agency:sec:cbi}) On the graphical production economy dynamic applied to routing shares has revenue as the evidence rate, while the price recursion equation is simultaneously backpropagation and Bellman recursion. Reward for a sub-agent is thus not arbitrary: it is the price generated by the economy as ``super-agent'', and \emph{price recursion is the rational theory of reward}, complementing Bayes (\cref{agency:sec:rationalize}).
\item The accompanying \texttt{marketgrad} library (\cref{agency:sec:library}) implements this theory and numerically verifies the identities therein.
\end{enumerate}
}
\end{contribution}

\section{Information asymmetry and scalable oversight}\label{intro:sec:oversight}

\begin{quote}
What is weighed is seldom what is truly valued. The skin of the orange hides its pulp.

---Yoram Barzel.
\end{quote}

Markets may be thought of as measuring devices for people's values and beliefs: to learn how much humans value something, put it on the market and see what it sells for. This leads us to naturally anticipate that economics/mechanism design solves alignment, with the natural economic proposal for human feedback to AI being ``AIs as producers, humans as consumers'' (with the goods being the information the AI provides or the task it completes for the user).

In fact, this framing helps us conceptualize the central problem of AI alignment\footnote{specifically \emph{outer} alignment, i.e. the problem of supplying the correct reward function that captures human preferences} in terms of the best-known failure mode of markets: \emph{information asymmetry}. When the buyer lacks information about some attributes of a good, those attributes do not affect his willingness to buy it---and thus the seller is not incentivized to optimize on these characteristics, leading to the ``market for lemons'' \citep{lemons}. Likewise in Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), when a human rater is not fully able to evaluate an AI agent's outputs (e.g. a response, or a task it completes for the user), the AI will only be trained on the human's immediate, superficial volition (to ``fool'' the buyer) rather than her \emph{extrapolated} volition\footnote{What the human buyer's preferences \emph{would} be if she had all the relevant information \citep{yudkowskyCoherentExtrapolatedVolition2004}.}.

The reason information asymmetry cannot be solved simply by ``purchasing information'' is that such an information market would, recursively, also suffer from information asymmetry. In fact information markets suffer from several fundamental flaws:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Buyer's inspection paradox.} Unlike with buying other goods, someone buying information definitionally does not \emph{know} what the information she is about to buy is\footnote{The seller could of course reveal part of the information, e.g. ``metadata'' to advertise the information---which creates a trade-off between information asymmetry and having positive externalities}. If you let the buyer bridge this asymmetry by inspecting the information before buying it, all incentives for trade break down because the buyer cannot ``return''/forget the information he has seen \citep{arrow1972economic,vanAlstyne1999AproposalValuingInformationInstrumentalGoods}.
    \item \textbf{Information asymmetry.} Even if the buyer did have some mechanism to inspect information before purchasing it (as is the case in RLHF, where the human rater sees the AI's outputs and still rates it correctly for exogenous reasons or the goodness of his heart), he might still be subject to information asymmetry just as in any market: just because you get to see a piece of information does not mean you can verify it.
    \item \textbf{Low cost of duplication.} Once you sell some information to someone, it is hard to prevent them from redistributing it. Ideally, the low marginal cost of production means information should have differential pricing, but the fact that others can also duplicate it makes this hard.
    \item \textbf{Transaction costs of tenders.} Often buying information involves ``tenders and proposals'': the buyer requests information, a bunch of sellers produce it, and the buyer selects the best one to buy. If you have the sellers just produce ``proposals'' then pick one to contract, then the seller will just swindle you (ignoring heuristic things like reputation). If you have the sellers produce everything upfront, then that will require a lot of sellers to go through the laborious process for no reward (and the buyer can now swindle the sellers). In practice this is why the white-collar labor economy doesn't run on freelancing but instead has things like employment and career progression.
    \item \textbf{Learning is a transaction cost.} Augmenting your intelligence is a form of information. However, the greatest cost in doing so is often not the price of the information itself, but the transaction cost of learning it.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{researchquestion}\label{rq:rlmf}
Can we design a mechanism for ``purchasing information from superhuman AIs'' that is robust to at least information asymmetry and the buyer's inspection paradox?
\end{researchquestion}

\begin{contribution}
Building on the work of \citet{weissRedesigningInformationMarkets2024}, we develop the \emph{Recursive Information Markets} (\cref{ch:infonomy}): a Bayesian framework for valuing information under recursive inspection by LLM buyers, in which the \emph{Recursive Inspection Protocol} is shown to be ex-ante superior to any admissible purchase protocol, together with a marginal-value reward mechanism for scalable oversight.
\end{contribution}

\begin{researchquestion}\label{rq:benchmark}

Many recent studies \citep{radhakrishnanAnthropicFall20232023,michael2023debateSuperviseUnreliableExperts,khanDebatingMorePersuasive2024,kentonScalableOversightWeak2024,arnesenTrainingLanguageModels2024} attempt to empirically evaluate Debate by measuring its effect on \emph{judge accuracy}, relative to a baseline of ``Random Consultancy'' (a protocol where the agent AI is randomly assigned an answer to argue for, and does not have an adversary). What would be a more principled approach to empirically evaluate and compare scalable oversight protocols?
\end{researchquestion}

\begin{contribution}
Noting that ``judge accuracy'' is the wrong metric from an alignment perspective---and introduce a principled approach to empirically evaluating scalable oversight mechanisms based on the \emph{Agent Score Difference} metric, which measures how much a protocol advantages truth over falsehood. We use this to establish the \emph{Scalable Oversight Mechanism benchmark} and accompanying library, and run demonstrative experiments on Debate, Consultancy and Propaganda protocols (\cref{ch:solib}).
\end{contribution}

Aligning and monitoring superhuman agents may often involve eliciting agent beliefs on tasks and sentences where we lack ``ground truth'': either because this is specifically the domain where scoring is non-trivial, or because it is relevant for interpretability. \draft{The economist's belief-elicitation devices --- proper scoring rules and prediction markets --- presuppose that ground truth eventually arrives to settle the bet; the questions that matter for alignment often do not.}

\begin{researchquestion}\label{rq:nonvf}
Prediction markets can easily give probabilities for verifiable or falsifiable (VF) events. But many questions we are interested in are not VF -- they exist only in the ``latent space''. Can we design scoring rules to incentivize usefully reporting probabilities on latent-space variables?
\end{researchquestion}

\begin{contribution}
We introduce a scoring mechanism for sentences that are \emph{neither verifiable nor falsifiable}, i.e. first-order logic sentences that are higher than Level 1 on the arithmetical hierarchy: after proving that no scoring rule or asset can do this directly, we construct \emph{ranged verification-falsification games} in which agents are scored through subgames on the VF instances a sentence quantifies over, and a program market that learns constructive truth-values in the limit (\cref{ch:nonvf}). The resulting economy may be seen as an AI that has learned some internal beliefs, and may be valuable for interpretability research \citep{christianoElicitingLatentKnowledge2021}.
\end{contribution}

\begin{researchquestion}\label{rq:consistency}
\draft{Even where ground truth exists, it may be too slow: an AI forecaster's predictions about the coming years cannot be scored today. Can forecasters be evaluated --- and improved --- \emph{before} resolution, using only the logical relationships between their forecasts?}
\end{researchquestion}

\begin{contribution}
We develop a consistency-based evaluation pipeline and principled, arbitrage-based inconsistency metrics for language model forecasters, which we then study empirically (\cref{ch:consistency}): inconsistency across logically related questions is measured as the profit an arbitrageur could extract from the forecaster, and a market-based ``\texttt{ArbitrageForecaster}'' corrects inconsistency in the way Platt scaling corrects miscalibration.
\end{contribution}
